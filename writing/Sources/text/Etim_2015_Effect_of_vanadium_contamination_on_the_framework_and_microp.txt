Abstract 
Y-zeolites are the main component of fluid catalytic cracking (FCC) catalyst for conversion of 
crude petroleum to products of high demand including transportation fuel. We investigated 
effects of vanadium which is present as one of the impurities in FCC feedstock on the framework 
and micropore structure of ultra-stable (US) Y-zeolite. The zeolite samples were prepared and 
characterized using standard techniques including: (1) X-ray diffraction, (2) N2 adsorption 
employing non local density functional theory method, NLDFT, (3) Transmittance and Pyridine 
FTIR, (4) Transmittance electron microscopy (TEM), and (5) 27Al and 29Si MAS-NMR. Results 
revealed that in the presence of steam, vanadium caused excessive evolution of non inter-
crystalline mesopores and structural damage. The evolved mesopore size averaged about 25.0 
nm at 0.5 wt.% vanadium loading, far larger than mesopore size in zeolitic materials with 
improved hydrothermal stability and performance for FCC catalyst. A mechanism of mesopore 
formation based on accelerated dealumination has been proposed and discussed. Vanadium 
immobilization experiments conducted to mitigate vanadium migration into the framework 
clearly showed vanadium is mobile at reaction conditions. From the results, interaction of 
vanadium with the passivator limits and decreases mobility, and acidity of vanadium into inner 
cavities of the zeolite capable of causing huge structure breakdown and acid sites destruction. 
This study therefore deepens insight into the causes of alteration in activity and selectivity of

vanadium contaminated catalyst and hints on a possible mechanism of passivation in vanadium 
passivated FCC catalyst.

1.0 
Introduction 
 
Zeolites are an important class of microporous, crystalline aluminosilicates with three-
dimensional pore structure widely used in various applications including catalysts [1-14].  Their 
use as catalysts for different conversion processes has unarguably underpinned the major 
application in petroleum refining, petrochemical and allied industries. In the case of petroleum 
refining, zeolite is quite indispensable. The discovery of zeolite catalyst in the 1960s has 
remained a major turnaround in the petroleum industry that has resulted in improved production 
and increased profitability [15]. Zeolite is exclusively responsible for cracking catalyst’s activity 
and Y-zeolite, having faujasite structure (Fig. 1), with well-defined pore network (7.4 x 7.4 Å) in 
its pure crystalline form, is the active component of fluid catalytic cracking (FCC) catalyst and it 
is used in acidic and rare earth forms to increase the yield of gasoline and diesel fuel from 
feedstock by cracking heavy paraffins into liquid fuels.

During FCC process zeolite catalysts are exposed to certain conditions that can modify important 
physiochemical properties such as aluminum content and acids strength. Such changes in zeolite 
composition and properties have significant impacts on catalytic activity, selectivity and stability. 
It has been established that the harsh FCC unit conditions, together with impurities present in the 
feedstock are directly linked with properties alterations [16, 17]. Amongst the impurities are 
metals including sodium [18], iron and calcium [19] and nickel and vanadium [20, 21]. However, 
nickel and vanadium are grievously detrimental to FCC catalyst’s performance [22-24].  
 
Specifically, behaviors of zeolites in the presence of steam and metal contaminants; 
sodium, vanadium and nickel have been studied. For instance, Pine [25] studied vanadium 
catalyzed destruction of USY zeolite and results showed steam has ability to destroy the zeolite 
system; however, his study was on siliceous zeolite. Vanadium and nickel interaction in REY 
had been reported. The authors discovered that during steam treatment in the presence of 
vanadium, dealumination of framework alumina and partial collapse of REY structure occur; 
vanadium also promoted coke formation of the zeolite. However, crystallinity of the zeolite

structure was preserved in the presence of nickel under similar conditions, but coke formation 
rate was generally on the increase [20, 21]. Tsiatouras and Evmiridis [24], in their study reported 
a complete structure breakdown of steam treated chromium-exchanged USY zeolite incorporated 
with vanadium. They authors added structure collapse was nearly proportional to dealumination, 
and impregnated vanadium selectively removed framework Al atoms. In a study to understand 
the influence of vanadium on the physiochemical and catalytic properties of USHY zeolite, 
Torrealba et al. [26] reported that vanadium decreased the stability of the zeolite through 
decrease in crystallinity and increase of dealumination rate during the hydrothermal treatment, 
and consequently the activity of the zeolite; but however, this decrease in activity was thought to 
be due partly to other phenomena, particularly to the exchange of the protonic sites by cationic 
vanadium species. Generally, vanadium deposited on the surface of the catalyst migrates into the 
zeolite structure in the presence of steam; however, matters concerning these facts are still 
debating and no comprehensive report is available in the literature on the action of vanadium on 
the pore-structure of USY zeolite. In view of the latter, a study of USY zeolite targeting the pore 
structure in the presence of a contaminating substance such as vanadium becomes necessary to 
fully comprehend causes of alteration in performance of vanadium contaminated Y-zeolite based 
catalyst. Results could find application in fabrication of better catalysts and improvement in 
general FCC catalyst technologies. In this report, we present the effects of vanadium 
contamination on the framework and micropore structure of USY zeolite using N2 adsorption by 
non-local density functional theory (NLDFT) method, powder XRD, MAS-NMR, FTIR and 
TEM. We have also demonstrated, using physiochemical properties, mitigation of vanadium 
poisoning of zeolite with a novel synthesized material.

2.0 
Experimental methods 
2.1 
Samples preparation 
 
Zeolite sample of this research, with unit cell size of 24.53 Å, Si/Al ratio 2.8 and sodium 
content (Na2O) of 0.42 wt.% was supplied by a catalyst manufacturing Company in Qingdao, 
China. Working sample was exchanged twice with 1.0 M NH4NO3 at 80 oC to remove sodium 
residue and calcined in air at 500 oC for 4 h. Vanadium USY (xV-USY), where “x” is weight 
percentage (wt.%) of vanadium in a given sample, were prepared by wet impregnation of 
vanadyl naphthenate dissolved in n-hexane. Excess organic solvent was evaporated freely in air 
and oven dried at 120 oC overnight. Oven dried samples were calcined in air at 550 °C for 5 h to 
obtained the test samples. Unless otherwise stated, all samples were steamed treated for 5 h at a 
temperature of 760 oC. Passivator as a separate particle was added to oven dried samples by 
physically admixing desirable amount before subjected to hydrothermal treatment. 
 
2.2 
Preparation of vanadium immobilizer  
 
We used yttrium-magnesium oxide (Y/MgO) as our immobilizer here in referred to as 
“passivator”. Five (5.0 ) g MgO was dispersed in 40 ml distilled water and stirred for 20 min. 
Yttrium nitrate (Y(NO3).6H2O) (4.75 g) dissolved in 10 ml distilled water was added and the 
mixture allowed stirring for another 30 min. After this time the white suspension formed was 
transferred to a water bath at 101 oC and allowed to stir until evaporation to dryness. The white 
solid obtained was oven dried overnight at 110 oC and calcined in air at 600 oC for 5 h.

2.3 
Characterizations 
 
Internal structure examination and patterns generation were carried out by powder X-ray 
diffraction (XRD) of the zeolite samples on a PAN analytical X’pert PRO (Germany), X-ray 
diffractometer using Cu-Kα radiation generated at 40 kV and 40 mA. A scanning range from 5° 
to 60° was used at a speed of 0.02° 2  /s. Crystallinity of the samples was determined using 
XRD according to standard method in ASTM 3906-03. MAS-NMR was measured for 27Al and 
29Si using a Bruker Advance III-400 MHz spectrometer. 27Al was measured at a frequency of 
104 MHz at a spinning rate of 12 kHz using a 4-mm probe head, single pulse length of 6 s 
( /20o) with AlK(SO4)2.H2O as standard. 29Si spectra was acquired at 80.4962 MHz at the same 
spinning rate with reference to DSS-(4,4-dimethyl-4-silapentane-1-sulfonic acid). Transmittance 
FTIR and acidity characterization were carried out on FTIR spectrophotometer, equipped with a 
MCT liquid nitrogen cooled detector and a KBr beam splitter. The spectra of the samples were 
recorded after 64 scans at 4 cm−1 resolution. Before analysis all samples were vacuum dried for 2 
h to eliminate adsorbed water and gaseous impurities. Samples for acidity studies were sorbed 
with pyridine for 24 h and the physisorbed species desorbed by vacuum drying at 150 oC for 2 h 
before analysis. The microstructure and morphology of the materials were examined by 
transmission electron microscopy, TEM (JEOL JEM-2100UHR) with an accelerating voltage of 
200 kV. Textural characterizations including surface area (calculated by BET method, relative 
pressure (P/Po) = 0.05–0.28) and pore volume (extrapolated from t-plot, P/Po = 0.98) were 
obtained by nitrogen physisorption method at 77 K following degassing of samples for 6 h at 300 
oC, using ASAP 2020 and Tristar 3000 surface area/porosity analyzer both from Micromeritics. 
Pore size distribution of the catalyst samples were calculated by non-local density functional 
theory (NLDFT) formalism using non-negative regularization. This method developed by

Textural characterization of materials is one of the most important practices for 
optimizing their applications. For instance, total surface area and porosity properties are 
important for catalyst’s performance since they determine accessibility of active sites and often 
relate to catalyst’s activity (surface area), while the pore structures including pore size 
distribution and pore volume control transport phenomena and regulate selectivity in catalytic 
reactions [39]. The effect of vanadium incorporation on the textural structural properties of the 
catalyst was investigated by analyzing the data from N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms. 
Nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherms (Fig. 3(I)) were obtained from ASAP 2020. The 
isotherms are a combination of types (near-type I & IV); type I, containing cylindrical-narrow 
pores typical of solids having exclusively micropore size with a quantity of mesopores (a) and 
type (IV), showing bimodal distribution of pores (micro-mesopores, (c–d)) respectively [31, 39]. 
The figure (Fig. 3(II)) (bottom) shows an entirely destroyed microporous zeolite material with a 
near parallel (zero) adsorption at P/Po ≤ 0.45. This represents a case of lost micropores leading to 
cavitations (large mesopores).

Pore size distribution analysis was performed using NLDFT formalism from 
experimental adsorption (N2 @ -196 oC) isotherms data and a model for cylindrical pores in 
microporous solids (method: non-negative regularization, medium smoothing with λ = 0.01). Fig. 
5 and 6 show the pore size distribution for the samples in full and micropore regions respectively. 
It can be seen for all samples there are peaks indicating pore size with average width less than or 
equal 1.0 nm in the micropore region. The observed average pore sizes are slightly higher than 
constitutional average pore size in conventional USY zeolite except in parent-USY which value 
is in very close agreement. The average pore width of the parent-USY is 0.73 nm as determined 
from our experiment, closed to 0.74 nm average pore width of the zeolite with faujasite structure. 
This value increased to 1.00 nm (DFT model) on incorporation of vanadium into the zeolite due 
to pore expansion. For average pore width less than 0.74 nm, it is not unusual as such 
observation had been reported by Saito and Foley [29] in their study to assess the value of the 
cylindrical pore model for predicting zeolite micro-PSD in Y-zeolites; it is indicative of ultra-
micropores. The magnitude of the peaks decreases and become broaden, corresponding to loss of 
pore volume and variation in pore size (Fig. 6). Nevertheless, another set of pore distribution 
(average ~25.3 nm) emerged at the region of pore width between 10 and 60 nm for vanadium 
poisoned samples, an indicative of presence of pores of larger size (mesopores). The incremental 
pore volume plots shown in Fig. 5 are in line with the adsorption isotherms in Fig. 3(I), and with 
the surface area and pore volume distribution data (Tables 2 and 3). These results reveal that

physisorption that provides micropore and mesopore volumes and pore size distribution, which 
the mesopores shape can be ascertained from the shape of physisorption isotherms [33]. 
However, transmission electron microscopy, in addition to elucidating cystallinity and phases, 
visualizes mesoporosity within a crystal and the bulk material [45, 46]. TEM images (Fig. 8) 
reveal clearly excessive holes (mesoporosity) in vanadium treated samples. Also indicated are

crystalline integrity of the sample with higher vanadium concentration is highly impaired. The

Based on our experimental results, we have proposed a mechanism for the formation of 
mesopores in vanadium contaminated zeolite. USY zeolites are designed to be thermally stable. 
In the presence of steam and some foreign substances the original stability is compromised. 
Steaming alone extracts few aluminum atoms from the zeolite framework and creates limited 
vacancies [56, 57]. Portion of the vacancies (cavitations) are healed by silicon mobile species, 
resulting in the formation of mesopores [58]. However, in the presence of vanadium, 
excessive/accelerated dealumination of framework aluminum atoms occurs requiring 
proportionate filling by silicon atoms. The detail is summarized in the equations below:

Under FCC regeneration conditions vanadium, which has been reported to exist in (+5) oxidation 
state [22, 25, 59] (V2O5), undergoes reaction to form mobile vanadic acid (H3VO4) on the surface 
of the zeolite. Vanadic acid formed undergoes intra-particle migration to the zeolite framework 
where it attacks and destroys strong acid sites, Si(OH)Al, via extraction of tetrahedral aluminum 
atoms in the zeolite crystal, causing it to collapse, and forms solid aluminum vandate (AlVO4) 
solution (Eq. 4). Due to unstable nature of AlVO4 under FCC conditions, it decomposes and

H3VO4 acid is re-formed to continue aluminum extraction (dealumination) process in a catalytic 
fashion (Eq. 5). According to this mechanism, vanadic acid would function as a catalyst since it 
is not consumed [17]. From the perception of the above mechanism and our XRD data (NAl), 
excessive dealumination accompanying vanadium contamination leaves much vacancies/defects 
(silanol nests) which are insufficiently healed by unstable mobile silicon species 
(dealumination >>> silicon substitution). As a result, cavitations formed from expelled 
aluminum and silicon mobile species are depicted as mesopores revealed from our TEM data. It 
is worth noting that the formed mesopores are non-intercrystalline, most of which are spherical, 
and some few which coalesce to larger pores through micropore openings having variant shapes.

4.0 
Conclusion  
 
Vanadium in the petroleum feedstock affects catalyst in several ways including loss of 
micropores, occupies the inner pores of zeolite, acid sites losses, accelerated dealumination 
leading to the formation of larger mesopores, and destroys the framework structure. Its 
contamination of a catalyst produced a continuous loss of textural properties, as a function of the 
quantity of the metal. At vanadium contents above catalyst tolerable level in petroleum 
feedstocks, excessive loss of crystallinity (microporosity) and introduction of large mesoporosity 
would result in collapse of zeolite structure. The instability of vanadium poisoned zeolite catalyst 
at FCC regeneration conditions is related not only to microporosity and framework degradations, 
but also to the formation of a material with different structure which lacks suitable acid sites and 
catalytic cracking properties. Vanadium immobilization experiment showed that mitigation of 
vanadium destructive effects preserved important catalytic properties such as acidity, 
crystallinity and surface area.
