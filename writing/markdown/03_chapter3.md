# 3.0 METHODOLOGY

## 3.1 Computational Design and Resources

A two-stage computational workflow was used. GFN2-xTB calculations screened initial structures and candidate reaction paths. Accepted geometries were then refined with density functional theory (DFT), and the adsorption energies were checked with a second functional. Figure 3.1 presented the workflow used in the calculations.

[FIG: fig3_1_workflow.png | Figure 3.1: Two-stage computational workflow used in this study | Source: author]

The GFN2-xTB calculations were run with ORCA 6.1.0 on a Dell Latitude E6430 laptop with 16 GB of memory and one processor core. The DFT calculations were run with ORCA 6.1.1 on a cloud virtual machine using four MPI processes (Neese, 2025). Python scripts in the repository parsed the ORCA output files and generated the calculation tables. Molecular structures were prepared and checked with Spartan and Avogadro.

## 3.2 Zeolite Y Cluster and Adsorbate Models

The Zeolite Y model was a gas-phase cluster with the formula AlSi₄O₄H₁₃. It contained one framework aluminium atom, four neighbouring silicon atoms, four bridging oxygen atoms, twelve terminal hydrogen atoms, and one proton on a bridging oxygen. The protonated oxygen formed the model Brønsted acid site. The terminal hydrogen atoms capped the bonds at the cluster boundary.

The twelve terminal hydrogen atoms were held fixed during geometry optimisation. The aluminium atom, four silicon atoms, bridging oxygen atoms, and Brønsted proton were allowed to relax. The cluster, vanadic acid, and water were treated as neutral singlets. Vanadic acid was modelled with one V=O bond and three V–OH groups; water was modelled as H₂O. Several initial adsorbate orientations were prepared around the acid site before screening.

The model represented the local acid site rather than the full Zeolite Y crystal. Figure 3.2 showed the optimised cluster and the two isolated adsorbates used as reference species. The same cluster was used for both adsorption calculations so that the energy comparison used a common framework model.

## 3.3 Initial Geometry Screening

The isolated cluster, vanadic acid, and water were first optimised with GFN2-xTB. Adsorption complexes were then generated by placing each adsorbate in different orientations near the Brønsted proton and relaxing the structures. The lowest-energy converged structures were carried forward for DFT refinement. This procedure provided starting geometries and did not establish accurate adsorption energies.

Candidate reaction paths were explored with nudged elastic band calculations followed by eigenvector-following transition-state optimisations. The searches covered the proposed vanadic-acid route and a water-hydrolysis control. Jobs that did not converge or failed the vibrational checks were retained in the calculation register but were not treated as validated structures.

## 3.4 DFT Refinement and Adsorption Energies

The accepted reference structures and adsorption complexes were re-optimised with B3LYP-D3(BJ)/def2-TZVP. The calculations used the RIJCOSX approximation, the def2/J auxiliary basis, the DefGrid3 integration grid, tight self-consistent-field convergence, and tight geometry convergence. Harmonic frequencies were calculated for the optimised structures at the same level of theory.

PBE0-D3(BJ)/def2-TZVP single-point calculations were performed on the B3LYP-optimised geometries of the isolated reference species and the two adsorption complexes. The geometries were not re-optimised with PBE0. This calculation provided a second estimate of the relative adsorption energies, rather than an independent geometry optimisation.

The electronic adsorption energy was calculated as:

ΔEₐdₛ = E(complex) − [E(cluster) + E(adsorbate)]
(3.1)

A negative value indicated that the complex had a lower electronic energy than the separated cluster and adsorbate. The relative energy difference was calculated as:

ΔΔE = ΔEₐdₛ(H₃VO₄) − ΔEₐdₛ(H₂O)
(3.2)

A negative ΔΔE indicated a stronger electronic adsorption energy for vanadic acid. The cluster reference energy cancelled from this difference. Counterpoise calculations were attempted to estimate basis-set superposition error, but they did not produce a complete set of converged fragment energies; no counterpoise-corrected values were used.

## 3.5 Vibrational Analysis and Thermochemistry

Harmonic frequencies were used to classify the optimised structures and calculate zero-point and thermal corrections. A minimum was accepted when the optimisation converged and no chemically meaningful imaginary frequency remained. Frequencies below 20 cm⁻¹ were treated as numerical noise when the associated displacement did not represent a chemical reaction. A transition-state candidate was accepted only when its optimisation converged, it had one chemically meaningful imaginary frequency, and displacement in both directions led to the two expected minima.

Gibbs free energies were calculated at 298.15 K and 1003.15 K using the ideal-gas, rigid-rotor, harmonic-oscillator treatment in ORCA. The second temperature represented a typical RFCC regenerator temperature of approximately 730 °C. The calculated thermochemical corrections were assessed separately from the electronic adsorption energies because the gas-phase entropy treatment did not include zeolite pore confinement.

## 3.6 Pathway Checks and Data Handling

Candidate structures beyond adsorption were assessed using geometry convergence, vibrational-frequency counts, relative energies, and displacement tests. A candidate transition state was not accepted if the optimisation failed, if it had more than one meaningful imaginary frequency, or if the displaced structures did not relax to the expected reactant and product minima. Energies from failed or unconverged jobs were retained for audit purposes but were excluded from mechanistic conclusions.

The ORCA input files, output files, final geometries, and calculation registers were retained in the repository. The results tables were checked against the output logs before they were entered in Chapter Four. This audit trail allowed each accepted value and each failed calculation to be traced to its source file.
