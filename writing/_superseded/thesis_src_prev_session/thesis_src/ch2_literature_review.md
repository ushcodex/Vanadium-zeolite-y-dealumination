# CHAPTER TWO

# LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 Introduction

This chapter reviews the literature from which the present study was built. It begins with the industrial setting, which is residue fluid catalytic cracking, and with the catalyst that the process depends on. It then narrows to the faujasite framework and the origin of its acidity, to the ways in which that framework fails, and to vanadium as the specific agent of failure. At that point the chapter reaches its centre of gravity, which is the disagreement in the published literature about what vanadium actually does. The chapter then reviews the experimental work that has defined the argument, the computational work that has been done on zeolite hydrolysis, and the methods available for extending that computational work to the vanadium case. It closes by identifying the gap that the present study fills.

## 2.2 Residue Fluid Catalytic Cracking

Fluid catalytic cracking converts heavy petroleum fractions into lighter products by bringing vaporised feedstock into contact with a hot, fine, acidic catalyst powder in a riser reactor, separating the spent catalyst from the products, and regenerating it by burning off the coke in air. The process accounts for a large share of global gasoline production, and no refinery of any size operates without it.

Residue fluid catalytic cracking is the variant that takes atmospheric residue as part or all of its feed. Adanenche et al. (2023) reviewed the technology and its constraints from the perspective of a Nigerian refining context. Their review notes that the RFCC catalyst must combine activity and selectivity with hydrothermal stability and a tolerance for the metals that concentrate in residues, and that the design of the reactor system has evolved around that requirement, with multi zone risers, two stage risers and auxiliary risers introduced to manage the severe conditions. The review also documents that over fifty countries operated FCC capacity of roughly fourteen million barrels per day by 2020, which gives the scale of the problem a useful frame: any catalyst design change that extends catalyst life by a modest fraction has a large absolute effect.

The relevant point for this study is that residue feeds carry metals, that those metals remain on the catalyst after the hydrocarbon has left, and that metal accumulation is cumulative over the life of the catalyst particle. Vanadium reaches loadings of several thousand parts per million on equilibrium catalyst in residue service, and at those loadings the catalyst loses crystallinity, surface area and activity.

## 2.3 Zeolite Y: Framework, Porosity and Acidity

Zeolite Y is the synthetic form of the mineral faujasite, and its structure is a three dimensional network of SiO₄ and AlO₄ tetrahedra linked through shared oxygen atoms. The tetrahedra are arranged in truncated octahedral cages called sodalite cages, and the sodalite cages are joined through hexagonal prisms into a diamond-like lattice that encloses large spherical cavities called supercages, each about 1.3 nanometres in diameter. The supercages are entered through twelve membered rings of about 0.74 nanometres free diameter, and it is through these rings that reactant molecules travel. Figure 2.1 shows this arrangement, with the pore opening of the twelve membered ring marked.

![Figure 2.1: The faujasite structure of zeolite Y, showing the average micropore width of the supercage formed by the twelve membered ring.](figures/lit/fig2_2_faujasite_supercage.png)

The aluminium atoms are the source of the catalytic function. Where silicon appears in the framework it is four coordinate and neutral, and the framework carries no charge. Where aluminium appears, the AlO₄ tetrahedron carries a net negative charge that must be balanced by a cation. When that cation is a proton attached to a bridging oxygen, the result is a Brønsted acid site of the form ≡Si–O(H)–Al≡, and it is this group that protonates a hydrocarbon and initiates cracking. The number and strength of these sites determine catalyst activity, so any process that removes aluminium from the framework, or replaces the proton with a metal cation, reduces activity directly.

Figure 2.2 contrasts a faujasite lattice in which all framework positions are occupied by aluminium with one from which a fraction of that aluminium has been extracted. In the second model the framework is visibly interrupted and a cavity has begun to open where the aluminium used to be. The distinction is the physical meaning of dealumination.

![Figure 2.2: Three dimensional atomistic models of the faujasite framework, (a) with all framework aluminium atoms present and (b) with part of the framework aluminium removed.](figures/lit/fig2_3_faujasite_atomistic.png)

Experimental work on the acidity of zeolite Y and of related frameworks has been complemented by periodic DFT calculations of Brønsted and Lewis acid sites. Tielens and Dzwigaj (2010) used periodic DFT together with experimental infrared spectroscopy of adsorbed pyridine to establish the protonation and deprotonation energies of vanadium-containing zeolite sites, and reported that vanadium bound hydroxyl groups are more acidic than silanol groups. That work concerned vanadium incorporated into the framework as part of the lattice, and it establishes that a vanadium oxygen hydrogen group can act as a strong acid. It does not address a vanadic acid molecule arriving from outside the lattice, which is the situation in a contaminated cracking catalyst.

## 2.4 Deactivation of Zeolite Y

Deactivation of the zeolite component of a cracking catalyst proceeds by several routes, and it is the irreversible routes that determine catalyst life. Coke deposition is reversible, because the carbon is burned off during regeneration. Framework breakdown is not.

Two irreversible processes dominate. The first is dealumination, the hydrolytic removal of framework aluminium, which eliminates Brønsted acid sites and leaves silanol nests in the places where aluminium used to sit. The second is desilication, the removal of framework silicon, which occurs more slowly but eventually destroys the crystallinity of the material outright. Both are hydrolytic, and both are accelerated by steam at the temperatures reached in a regenerator.

Malola et al. (2012) provided the first complete first principles treatment of both processes. Working with the chabazite framework in a periodic cell and using the nudged elastic band method to locate transition states, they treated dealumination as a sequence of hydration reactions requiring at least three water molecules to form a silanol nest and detach aluminium in the form of Al(OH)₃, with a fourth water molecule stabilising the detached species as Al(OH)₃(H₂O). Their reaction sequence is set out in the following equations, in which the framework is written in terms of its tetrahedral atoms:

Step 1: ≡Si–O–Si≡ + H₂O → 2 ≡Si–OH                     (2.1)

Step 2: inversion of a hydroxyl group and reorientation of the bonds  (2.2)

Step 3: ≡Si–O–Al≡ + H₂O → ≡Si–OH + HO–Al≡              (2.3)

Step 4: second hydration gives four loosely bound hydroxyl groups, two on silicon and two on aluminium  (2.4)

Step 5: third hydration gives partially bonded Al(OH)₃    (2.5)

Step 6: adsorption of a fourth water molecule detaches Al(OH)₃(H₂O)  (2.6)

Their calculated overall barrier for dealumination was 190 kJ mol⁻¹ when the energy of water adsorption is counted at each step, and 260 kJ mol⁻¹ when it is not, with the rate determining step being the inversion of the hydroxyl group. The corresponding figures for desilication were 40 to 50 kJ mol⁻¹ higher, from which they concluded that dealumination is the less activated of the two processes in steam. Figure 2.3 reproduces the reaction profile they computed.

![Figure 2.3: Reaction paths for (a) dealumination and (b) desilication, combined from five nudged elastic band paths. Black lines describe initially isolated water molecules and purple lines describe initially adsorbed water molecules. Effective barriers are labelled in the upper left corner.](figures/lit/fig2_4_malola_paths.png)

Figure 2.4 shows the corresponding sequence of intermediate structures. The figure is important to the present work for a methodological reason as well as a chemical one: it establishes that in steam dealumination the reacting water adsorbs on the aluminium atom in the position anti to the Brønsted acid site before any bond breaks. That is the geometry that any incoming molecule must compete with.

![Figure 2.4: Reaction steps with intermediate configurations for dealumination on the left and desilication on the right. Covalent bonds are marked and hydrogen bonds are marked.](figures/lit/fig2_5_malola_steps.png)

Silaghi et al. (2016) extended this picture across four frameworks, mordenite, faujasite, MFI and chabazite, using periodic DFT with dispersion corrections and free energy estimation. They confirmed the general mechanism, identified the anti position of the adsorbing water as a universal feature, and showed that confinement of the extra framework aluminium species inside the zeolite cavity is itself a thermodynamic driving force for extraction. They also established Brønsted Evans Polanyi relationships between the heat of water adsorption and the activation energy for hydrolysis, although they noted that these relationships degrade as more aluminium oxygen bonds are hydrolysed. The faujasite case in their study is directly relevant here, because it confirms that the framework of the catalyst used in this work hydrolyses by the same mechanism as the others.

The temperature dependence of the process was addressed by Nielsen et al. (2019), who used enhanced sampling molecular dynamics to follow dealumination in the presence of a realistic water loading, and by Stanciakova et al. (2019), who examined the cooperation between water molecules in the initial stage. Nielsen and co-workers estimated a loading of more than three water molecules per unit cell at steaming conditions of 450 °C and one bar of steam, and found that additional water molecules lower the free energy barrier by participating in proton shuttling. Their intrinsic barriers for the single water pathway clustered near 100 kJ mol⁻¹, in contrast to the much wider spread obtained from static calculations, which they attributed to the difficulty of applying harmonic approximations at high temperature. Figure 2.5 summarises the resulting view of the process: water is not a passive spectator but a cooperative participant, and barriers computed for a single molecule are upper bounds.

![Figure 2.5: Illustration of the mechanisms of vanadium poisoning of cracking catalysts, set within the broader deactivation picture.](figures/lit/fig2_7_vanadium_poisoning_scheme.png)

The consequence for the present work is a benchmark. Any statement that vanadic acid attacks the faujasite framework more aggressively than steam must be measured against a steam barrier of roughly 100 to 260 kJ mol⁻¹ depending on the treatment, and against a steam adsorption energy on the aluminium site of the order of tens of kilojoules per mole.

## 2.5 Vanadium in the Cracking Unit

Vanadium enters the unit bound in porphyrin and asphaltene structures in the residue feed. It leaves the reactor deposited on the catalyst as vanadyl species and, after regeneration, as vanadium pentoxide. Vanadium pentoxide melts at about 690 °C, which is within the regenerator temperature window, so the deposited oxide is a mobile liquid rather than a fixed solid. Above a certain loading the oxide forms a bulk phase on the catalyst surface; below it, the vanadium is dispersed as a surface vanadate.

The chemistry that follows from that mobility was set out by Wormsbecher et al. (1986), who identified the poison precursor as volatile vanadic acid formed by the reaction of vanadium pentoxide with steam, and estimated its concentration in a typical regenerator at one to ten parts per million. They drew the analogy with phosphoric acid, which is of comparable acid strength, and concluded that vanadic acid destroys the zeolite by hydrolysis of the SiO₂/Al₂O₃ framework. Their proposed remedy, which has shaped industrial practice ever since, was to add a basic solid with a suitable pore structure to scavenge the acid before it reaches the zeolite, and they demonstrated activity retention with magnesia.

The state and behaviour of vanadium on cracking catalysts was reviewed in detail by Occelli (1991), who brought together the electron paramagnetic resonance, diffuse reflectance and catalytic evidence on vanadium speciation and concluded that the interaction of vanadium with the zeolite is governed by the oxidation state of the metal and by the availability of steam. The experimental method by which such samples are prepared, in which a fresh catalyst is heat shocked and then impregnated to incipient wetness with metal naphthenates before steam treatment, was established by Mitchell (1980) and remains the basis of the contamination procedures used in the more recent studies cited here. Figure 2.6 shows the result. Vanadium migration increases markedly as the calcination temperature rises, and it moves both between catalyst particles and within them, which is why a vanadium trap must be distributed through the catalyst particle rather than merely mixed with it.

![Figure 2.6: Effect of calcination temperature on the mobility of vanadium species.](figures/lit/fig2_6_vanadium_mobility.png)

Trujillo et al. (1997) studied the state of vanadium on the zeolite using electron spin resonance, diffuse reflectance spectroscopy and sorption measurements. They concluded that vanadium deposited on the external surface migrates into the channels when the zeolite is heated in an oxidising atmosphere, that water assists the migration but is not required for it, and that vanadium is stabilised as the VO₂⁺ cation near the acid sites. Their central chemical proposal is that the cation reacts with water inside the zeolite according to

VO₂⁺–Y + 2H₂O ⇌ H⁺–Y + H₃VO₄                       (2.7)

which returns the exchange site to its protonated form while releasing vanadic acid into the pore system. Vanadic acid, being a strong acid, then hydrolyses the framework. In this scheme vanadium is a catalyst rather than a reagent, because it is regenerated at the end of each cycle. Trujillo and co-workers also reproduced the classical scheme of Kerr for zeolite dealumination alongside an electrophilic attack of the hydronium ion on the negatively charged oxygen of the acid site, which is shown in Figure 2.7. That figure is reproduced here because it makes explicit the point that the framework hydrolysis reaction is an acid catalysed process, and therefore that any acid capable of reaching the site can in principle promote it.

![Figure 2.7: Kerr's scheme for zeolite dealumination, upper scheme, together with the electrophilic attack of the hydronium ion on the negatively charged oxygen of the acid site, lower scheme.](figures/lit/fig2_9_trujillo_scheme.png)

At the level of the catalyst particle, Etim et al. (2016) quantified the damage. Working with ultra stable zeolite Y contaminated at loadings between 0 and 1 wt% and steamed at 760 °C, they measured crystallinity by X-ray diffraction, pore structure by nitrogen adsorption interpreted with non-local density functional theory, acidity by pyridine infrared spectroscopy and framework coordination by magic angle spinning nuclear magnetic resonance. They found that vanadium in the presence of steam caused the growth of mesopores averaging about 25 nanometres at 0.5 wt% vanadium loading, far larger than the mesopores found in hydrothermally stable cracking catalysts, and that a sample loaded at 1 wt% lost its zeolite phases entirely. They also showed that immobilising vanadium on a yttrium magnesium oxide passivator reduced its mobility and preserved the framework, which is a direct demonstration that the damage depends on where the vanadium goes rather than merely on how much is present.

## 2.6 The Disputed Mechanism

The literature reviewed so far presents vanadium as the direct cause of framework destruction. That reading was challenged from two directions, and the challenge is the reason this study exists.

The first challenge came from Pine (1990), who measured the kinetics of USY zeolite destruction by vanadium and by sodium in steam between 740 and 800 °C over a vanadium range of 0 to 4000 parts per million. Pine found that vanadium and sodium are nearly equal in activity as destruction catalysts and that their effects are synergistic. Critically, he also found that vanadium readily attacks silicalite, a zeolite that contains essentially no aluminium. That observation is incompatible with a mechanism in which the attack is directed exclusively at aluminium oxygen bonds, because there is no aluminium to attack. Pine proposed instead that vanadium and sodium both act on surface hydroxyl groups in the step that controls the rate of nucleation, and that the attack by steam can therefore involve silicon oxygen bonds. His measured apparent activation energy for destruction by vanadium was 330.5 kJ mol⁻¹ and by sodium 320.5 kJ mol⁻¹, values so large that they suggest a very stable framework and an attack that is difficult rather than facile.

The second challenge came from Xu et al. (2002), who proposed an explicit mechanism linking sodium and vanadium and remains the most direct statement of the opposing position. Their argument is that sodium reacts with steam to form a surface sodium hydroxide, that the basic hydroxide ion attacks framework silicon oxygen bonds, and that this is the destructive step. Vanadium does not open a new route; its role is to react with cationic sodium at the exchange site, releasing the sodium from the zeolite, and the sodium metavanadate so formed hydrolyses in steam to regenerate sodium hydroxide and metavanadic acid. In their words, the mechanism of Y zeolite destruction remains the same whether vanadium is present or not, and without sodium, vanadium itself has little effect on Y zeolite stability regardless of the unit cell size of the zeolite. Their experimental evidence is reproduced in Figure 2.8, which shows the hydrothermal stability of catalysts with different sodium contents in the presence and absence of vanadium. The lines separate according to sodium content rather than according to vanadium content.

![Figure 2.8: Effect of vanadium on the hydrothermal stability of catalysts. Steaming conditions were 1060 K, 1 atm, 90% steam in air, for 4 h.](figures/lit/fig2_8_xu_hydrothermal_stability.png)

A third position has since emerged, and it is the strongest form of the challenge. Liu et al. (2019) noted that the model zeolites used by Xu and co-workers had high framework silicon to aluminium ratios and low unit cell sizes, and argued that such frameworks are intrinsically more resistant to acid catalysed hydrolysis, which would make the absence of a vanadium effect in those samples unrepresentative. Working with USY containing a defined sodium content and vanadium loadings up to about 1.12 wt%, and treating the samples in steam at elevated temperature, Liu and co-workers found severe damage to the framework, sharp deterioration in residue cracking and noticeable changes in gasoline composition, with almost all characteristic zeolite diffraction peaks disappearing at the highest loading. They concluded that high deposited vanadium on sodium-containing USY is strongly destructive in steam. The report of Sandoval-Díaz and co-workers, cited by Liu et al. (2019) as having found destruction independent of vanadium in the presence of sodium, is at the opposite extreme; the two studies disagree even though both work in the presence of sodium.

The dispute, reduced to its essentials, is whether the attacking entity is vanadic acid derived from vanadium or hydroxide derived from sodium, and whether the bond broken is an aluminium oxygen bond or a silicon oxygen bond. The present study addresses the first half of that question directly and the second half only by implication, because the cluster used here contains one aluminium and four silicon tetrahedral sites, and the reacting coordinate followed is the one involving aluminium.

Two recent reviews frame the contest without resolving it. Xu et al. (2024) surveyed the poisoning behaviour of nickel, vanadium, iron and sodium on cracking catalysts and reported that the toxicity of vanadium manifests mainly as an alteration of catalyst texture leading to destruction of the molecular sieve structure, without committing to a single molecular mechanism. Adanenche et al. (2023) reviewed mitigation strategies and noted that the interaction mechanism between vanadium capture agents and vanadium has not been fully understood, which is an indirect statement of the same gap: if the mechanism of attack were settled, the design of a trap would follow from it.

## 2.7 Vanadium Traps and Passivators

Because the mechanism is contested, the engineering response to vanadium has been empirical, and a brief review of that response is useful because it shows what a computed binding energy would contribute.

Wormsbecher et al. (1986) proposed basic alkaline earth oxides on the reasoning that a base would neutralise an acid, and demonstrated magnesia. Etim et al. (2018) prepared a mixed magnesium and yttrium oxide and showed by X-ray diffraction that mobile vanadic acid reacts preferentially with the yttrium oxide, forming crystalline yttrium vanadate, so that the trap protects the catalyst by converting a mobile acid into an immobile salt. Their work also established that a definite quantity of trap material is necessary for optimum performance, and that incorporating the trap before the catalyst is metallated gives better protection than adding it afterwards. Liu et al. (2019) reported that lanthanum oxide introduced by simple physical mixing preserves the zeolite structure to a large extent, and attributed the effect to the preference of lanthanum oxide to interact with vanadium species rather than migrate into the zeolite channels. More recent work has extended the search to barium titanate (Faghani et al., 2024).

Alumina is the other traditional trapping phase, and its effectiveness depends on which crystalline form is present, because the transitional aluminas differ greatly in surface area and in acid base character. Bawa et al. (2017) studied the transformation of gamma alumina prepared from Kankara kaolin through ammonium alum, a route of local interest, and found that the gamma phase forms at 825 degrees Celsius, remains stable to 1125 degrees Celsius, and provides a specific surface area of 166 square metres per gram before transforming to the alpha phase above 1150 degrees Celsius. A support that retains its surface area through the regenerator temperature range is a prerequisite for a trap that must remain accessible to the migrating vanadium species.

Every one of these designs is a competition: the trap must react with the mobile vanadium species more readily than the zeolite does. Yet the quantity against which it competes, the binding energy of vanadic acid on a zeolite acid site, has not been computed. That is a specific and usable gap.

## 2.8 Computational Approaches to Zeolite Reactivity

The methods available for a study of this kind differ in cost and in the physical effects they capture, and the choice made in this work needs to be justified against the alternatives.

Periodic density functional theory is the reference standard for zeolite reactivity. It represents the crystal as an infinite repeating cell, so the confinement of the pore is included, and it has produced the definitive studies of steam dealumination reviewed above. Its cost is substantial, and a single nudged elastic band calculation on a framework transition state can require hundreds of processor hours. Hybrid functionals, which are needed for reliable thermochemistry, are considerably more expensive again in a periodic setting.

Cluster models occupy the other end of the range. A fragment of the framework, terminated so that the dangling bonds are saturated, is treated as a molecule. The approach is computationally inexpensive, it permits hybrid functionals and large basis sets at modest cost, and it allows transition states to be located with the standard molecular toolkit. Its weakness is the finite size of the model. Too small a cluster distorts the geometry of the reacting site, and the absence of the surrounding lattice removes the mechanical constraint that would otherwise resist deformation. The two principal side effects are underestimation of barrier heights and overestimation of adsorption energies. Cluster models remain widely used for exactly the reason they are used here, which is that they make a hybrid functional affordable for a comparative study.

Semi-empirical quantum chemical methods occupy the space between molecular mechanics and density functional theory. The GFN2-xTB method of Bannwarth et al. (2019) is a self-consistent tight binding treatment that includes multipole electrostatics up to quadrupole terms and a density dependent dispersion correction, and it was parameterised across the periodic table up to radon. Bannwarth et al. (2021) reviewed the extended tight binding family and reported that GFN2-xTB provides a favourable accuracy to cost ratio for geometries, frequencies and non-covalent interaction energies. The method is not accurate enough to report reaction energies as final results, but it is accurate enough to locate candidate stationary points on a potential energy surface and to screen a large number of starting geometries, after which the promising ones can be refined by DFT. The procedure is now standard practice in the field (Uzochukwu et al., 2023).

For the density functional itself, two choices are defensible. B3LYP, a hybrid functional combining Becke's three parameter exchange with the Lee, Yang and Parr correlation functional, is the most widely used hybrid for zeolite and organic systems, and it is well validated for reaction energies when combined with an empirical dispersion correction. Its known weakness is a tendency to underestimate reaction barriers. PBE0, the parameter free hybrid of Adamo and Barone (1999), is constructed from the Perdew, Burke and Ernzerhof exchange and correlation functional with exact exchange added in a fixed proportion, and it has been shown in the original study and subsequently to give balanced performance for thermochemistry and barrier heights. Using both, as this work does, provides an internal check on whether a computed result is an artefact of one functional.

## 2.9 The Gap in the Literature

Five observations can be drawn from the literature reviewed in this chapter.

First, the experimental literature does not agree on the mechanism by which vanadium destroys zeolite Y. Three distinct positions are defended, and the most recent contribution reports damage at high vanadium loading that the earlier, sodium-focused studies would not predict.

Second, the disagreement is about molecular events that experimental methods cannot observe directly. Diffraction, physisorption and microscopy describe the consequences of framework failure; they do not report the energy of an aluminium oxygen bond breaking in the presence of a particular adsorbate.

Third, the computational literature has produced a thorough description of framework hydrolysis by steam, including the reaction steps, the barriers across several framework types, and the cooperative role of additional water molecules. That description gives a firm benchmark for any competing pathway.

Fourth, there is no equivalent computational treatment of vanadic acid as an attacking species. The published DFT work on vanadium in zeolites concerns vanadium occupying framework positions in the lattice, which is a different chemical situation from a vanadic acid molecule arriving from the pore system.

Fifth, the industrial response to vanadium, which is the addition of a trap material, is a competition for vanadium between the trap and the zeolite, and the zeolite side of that competition has not been quantified.

The present study addresses the fourth and fifth observations and speaks to the first. It computes the adsorption of vanadic acid on a faujasite Brønsted acid site and compares it, on identical terms, with the adsorption of water, using a hierarchy of methods in which the semi-empirical level is used to find the structures and the density functional levels are used to judge them. The comparison is stated as an energy difference, which is the form in which it can be used by someone designing a trap, and it is tested against two density functionals so that the reader can see how much of the answer is method dependent.
