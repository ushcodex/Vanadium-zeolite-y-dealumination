# 1.0 INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Background of the Study

Residue fluid catalytic cracking (RFCC) converts heavy refinery feedstocks into lighter products. Its catalyst contains Zeolite Y, whose Brønsted acid sites support cracking reactions. Vanadium and other metals in residue feed accumulate on the catalyst during operation. Their accumulation is associated with loss of Zeolite Y activity during regeneration (Adanenche et al., 2023; Wormsbecher, Peters, & Maselli, 1986).

Steam hydrolyses framework Al–O–Si bonds during regeneration and removes framework aluminium. Each framework aluminium atom is associated with a Brønsted acid site; dealumination therefore reduces the number of sites available for cracking (Malola, Svelle, Lønstad Bleken, & Swang, 2012). This loss of acid sites links framework damage to catalyst activity.

Three explanations describe vanadium-related damage. Wormsbecher et al. (1986) proposed that vanadium oxide reacts with steam to form volatile vanadic acid, which attacks the zeolite. Pine (1990) attributed the main framework attack to steam and assigned vanadium a catalytic or secondary role. Xu, Liu, and Madon (2002) proposed a sodium-mediated route in which vanadium mobilises sodium and steam enables sodium hydroxide attack. These explanations focus on vanadic acid, steam, and sodium, respectively.

Wormsbecher et al. (1986) estimated low-parts-per-million vanadic-acid levels for a high-steam regenerator at about 2 atm. Water therefore occupies a much larger fraction of the bulk gas under those conditions. Comparing both adsorbates at the same model acid site clarifies their electronic and thermochemical preferences within this environment.

## 1.2 Statement of the Problem

Vanadium contamination is associated with accelerated loss of Zeolite Y activity, while the molecular explanation remains debated. The proposed mechanisms assign different roles to vanadic acid, steam, and sodium (Pine, 1990; Trujillo et al., 1997; Xu et al., 2002; Wormsbecher et al., 1986). A common-site comparison of vanadic acid and water provides a focused way to evaluate their relative adsorption and thermochemical preferences.

The present work examined these adsorbates at one Zeolite Y Brønsted acid site and assessed candidate pathways for framework Al–O cleavage. The resulting adsorption energies, pathway assessment, and high-temperature site-occupation estimate provide molecular-level evidence for interpreting the mechanisms reviewed in Chapter Two. These measures separate molecular association from the chemical steps involved in framework damage.

## 1.3 Aim and Objectives of the Study

The aim was to compare vanadic acid and water at a model Zeolite Y Brønsted acid site and assess their implications for dealumination.

The objectives were to:

1. construct and optimise a local Zeolite Y cluster and the initial vanadic-acid and water adsorption structures using ORCA;
2. calculate and compare the electronic adsorption energies of the selected vanadic-acid and water structures;
3. assess candidate pathways for the first framework Al–O cleavage step in the vanadic-acid route and a water-hydrolysis control; and
4. calculate harmonic Gibbs energies at 298.15 K and 1003.15 K and evaluate conditional competitive site occupation using RFCC gas partial pressures from the literature.

## 1.4 Scope and Limitations

The calculations used a gas-phase AlSi₄O₄H₁₃ cluster representing one Zeolite Y Brønsted acid site. Vanadic acid (H₃VO₄) and water (H₂O) were compared at this site. GFN2-xTB screened initial structures and pathway candidates; selected structures were optimised at B3LYP-D3(BJ)/def2-TZVP, and PBE0-D3(BJ)/def2-TZVP single-point energies checked the electronic comparison. Harmonic Gibbs energies were evaluated at 298.15 K and 1003.15 K, and the high-temperature values were combined with literature gas partial pressures in a single-site equilibrium estimate.

The model describes a local acid-site environment, with terminal hydrogen atoms fixed at the cluster boundary. Its thermochemical estimates use ideal-gas standard states, one equivalent adsorption site, and independent adsorbates. Pore confinement, local vanadium enrichment, and exchangeable sodium define extensions for future models. Appendix A provides the calculation register, and Appendix B gives the numerical substitutions behind the reported energies and site fractions.

## 1.5 Justification of the Study

A matched comparison at one model acid site separates electronic binding from harmonic thermochemical preference. This distinction helps interpret the proposed direct vanadic-acid interaction alongside the high steam abundance of an RFCC regenerator. The pathway assessment and conditional site-occupation estimate also identify the molecular quantities most relevant to larger-pore models and experimental comparison (Adanenche et al., 2023; Etim et al., 2018).
