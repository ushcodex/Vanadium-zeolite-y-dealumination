Research Highlights 
 A mixed metal oxide is studied as vanadium passivator for the FCC process 
 Vanadium deactivates FCC catalysts by both intra and inter particles migration mechanisms 
 Passivation of vanadium proceeds through acid-base chemistry principle by formation of 
vanadate of the passivator stable to vanadium attack under FCC unit conditions 
 A definite amount of passivator material is necessary for maximum performance 
 Incorporation of passivator before the metallation provides the best practice 
 
Abstract 
Vanadium scavenging material comprising magnesium and yttrium oxides (Mg-Y-oxide) was 
prepared and evaluated for vanadium capture under simulated FCC regeneration conditions for 
typical FCC process. Vanadium trapping ability was measured using BET, XRD, FTIR, EDX 
coupled SEM and MAT. The results show that physiochemical properties, vanadium trapping and 
catalyst protection increase with amount of the trap material. SEM-EDX results confirm migration 
of vanadium as indicated by its presence in the passivator particle. Vanadium migrates within the 
catalyst particles by both inter and intra particles mechanisms. The XRD data further reveal high 
vanadium selectivity towards yttrium when passivator is incorporated after impregnation of 
vanadium, with the formation of crystalline yttrium vanadate (YVO4). Catalytic evaluation 
indicates improved activities of catalysts containing passivator which correlate with 
physiochemical parameters. In the passivation mechanism, mobile vanadic acid preferentially 
reacts more with Y2O3 in mixed metal oxide due to its higher activity (active sites), forming YVO4. 
These results therefore reveal that the passivator protects the catalyst by formation of immobile

vanadate with mobile vanadic acid, thereby reducing hydrolysis of the catalyst framework and thus 
preserving important properties of the catalyst.

1.0 
Introduction 
Fluid catalytic cracking (FCC) is an important process in the petroleum refinery. It is responsible 
for 35-45 % gasoline pool in a typical refinery [1-3], and represents the heart of the refinery for 
production of light fuels [4]. The FCC process involves contacting heavy oil fractions such as 
vacuum gas oil at high temperature in the presence of a catalyst [5]. This high temperature reaction 
results in breaking down of large hydrocarbon molecules to lighter fraction of gasoline and diesel, 
as well as gaseous fuel (liquefied petroleum gas, LPG) and other petrochemical products [6, 7]. 
Since FCC is a versatile process and can process several feedstock types, including the abundant 
feeds that are highly contaminated with organic metals such as vanadium and nickel, it has 
therefore gained prominence in the petroleum industry. The effects of these metals that are present 
in the FCC feedstock on the activity and selectivity of the cracking catalyst have been widely 
studied [8-11]. Vanadium is detrimental to catalyst stability and structure, such as the crystallite, 
whereas nickel promotes dehydrogenation reactions and is selective to coke and lighter gases in 
lieu of high demand fuel. During regeneration process when coked catalyst is burned off in the 
regenerator with hot air, vanadium species deposited on the catalyst migrate under hydrothermal 
conditions and destroy the catalyst structure [12]. This presents a problem of growing concern to 
the refiner. Wombsbercher et al. [8], noted that vanadium deposited on the catalyst oxidizes to 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

<<<PAGE 5>>>
vanadium (V) oxide and reacts with water vapor present in the regenerator to form vanadic acid as 
shown in Eq. 1. 
V2O5 + 3H2O → 2H3VO4 
 
 
(1) 
As a strong acid, it then reacts with the zeolite catalyst, destroying its structure and activity. 
Whichever route vanadium penetrates the cracking catalyst, it poisons, leading to the death of the 
catalyst’s active life. For the fact that vanadium compounds cannot be completely removed by pre-
treating feedstock or may be a very difficult task altogether, a probable method for controlling 
vanadium negative effects on the cracking catalyst is to passivate vanadium compounds under 
typical FCC conditions. Passivation may involve incorporating additives into the cracking catalyst 
or adding separate particles along with the cracking catalyst [12, 13]. The use of alkaline earth 
metals such as Mg, Ca and Ba or rare earth metals as passivators for vanadium have been reported 
to retain activity and improve selectivity of catalyst during processing of feeds with high metal 
contents [14]. However, in spite of the good trapping capabilities of alkaline earth metals, there 
still exist problems; the formation of silicate reduces activity for the contaminant metal [12]. For 
example, magnesium oxide reacts with silicate to form crystalline magnesium silicate according 
to Eq. 2. 
2MgO + SiO2 → Mg2SiO4 
 
 
(2) 
The formation of crystalline metal silicate can be controlled by modifying MgO slurry to preserve 
the bulk MgO [12]. In this work, commercial MgO was modified with yttrium, a rare earth element, 
and applied to the passivation of vanadium effects on FCC catalyst. An effort is been made to 
understand/describe the mechanism of passivation of FCC catalyst by the new metal composite 
used as passivator in this study. The mechanism of passivation is discussed in terms of acidity and 
interaction of the passivator particles with the catalyst. A correlation between structural parameters

and activity of protected catalysts is also studied. It is believed that these results could provide new 
parameters for predicting deactivation of contaminated FCC catalyst.  
2. 
Experimental methods 
2.1 
Materials and sample preparation 
 
A commercial FCC catalyst, containing REY with surface area; 234 m2/g, total pore 
volume; 0.20 cm3/g, and RE2O3; 5.20 wt.% was used in the present study. Other components of 
the fresh catalyst (%) include: Na2O - 0.17; Al2O3 - 51.61; SiO2 - 24.23; Ni - 0.0117 and V - 0.0203. 
Vanadium was introduced as vanadium naphthenate containing 2.0 wt.% of the metal, dissolved 
in n-hexane. The catalyst sample was impregnated to a 0.75 wt.% V (nominal amount). In detail, 
for a 10.0 g catalyst, 3.75 g of vanadium naphthenate was dissolved in 2.0 mL n-hexane and 
impregnated to 9.925 g fresh FCC catalyst, dried in the oven at 120 °C and calcined in air at 500 °C 
for 6 h. The calcined sample was thermally treated in 100% steam at 760 °C for 5 h. A mixed metal 
composite used as passivator, containing magnesium and yttrium oxides, was prepared by our new 
facile method. Briefly, 5.0 g MgO was dispersed in 40 ml distilled water and stirred for 20 min. 
Yttrium nitrate (Y(NO3).6H2O) (4.75 g) dissolved in 10 ml distilled water was added and the 
mixture allowed stirring for another 30 min at room temperature. After this time, the white 
suspension formed was transferred to a water bath at 101 oC and allowed to stir until evaporation 
to dryness. The white solid obtained was oven dried overnight at 110 oC and calcined in air at 600 
oC for 5 h. The passivator was made to 60–80 µm particle size distributions, similar to FCC catalyst 
particles. The vanadium passivated catalyst samples were prepared by doping different dosages of 
the passivator, (3-10 wt.%) as (𝑥) wt.% Y-Mg-oxide, to a fixed weight of catalyst impregnated to 
0.75 wt.% V content and oven dried at 120 °C. Two control samples, passivator incorporated 
before impregnation and after calcinations at 550 °C were also prepared.

2.2 
Characterization 
 
The XRD patterns of the samples were obtained on PAN analytical X’pert PRO, X-ray 
diffractometer using Cu-Kα radiation generated at 40 kV and 40 mA. The 2𝜃 range was scanned 
from 5 to 60° with slit parameters. Surface areas and pore size distribution (PSD) were measured 
using a Micromeritics Tristar 3000 surface area and porosity analyzer at liquid nitrogen 
temperature. All the samples were degassed at 300 °C under vacuum for 4 h before analysis. The 
specific surface areas were calculated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) model [15]. The 
total volume was calculated from the amounts of nitrogen adsorbed at P/Po = 0.98. The micropore 
volume and the external surface area were determined from the t-plot analysis. The PSD of the 
samples were calculated using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method from adsorption branch 
[16]. The morphologies of vanadium passivated catalysts were measured with an S-4800 scanning 
electron microscope (SEM, Japan HQ) combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry 
(EDX) accelerating at 0.5–30 kV. Prior to the measurements, the samples were coated with a thin 
layer of gold. The types of acid sites on the surface of the oxides of the passivator were determined 
by transmission FTIR spectroscopy of pyridine adsorbed oxides on a Nicolet 6700 spectrometer 
equipped with a MCT liquid nitrogen cooled detector and a KBr beam splitter. The samples after 
adsorption of pyridine vapour for 24 h and desorption under vacuum at 0.09 MPa and 150 oC were 
put into a sample cup (dimension 10 mm and 2.3 mm) and inserted into the measurement chamber. 
The spectra of the samples were recorded by accumulating 64 scans at 4 cm-1 resolution adopting 
reflectance mode in the mid-infrared range (1400 to 1600 cm-1). Reported spectra were subtracted 
from the background of respective samples.

Results and discussion 
3.1 
Structural properties of passivator 
An effective trap for vanadium must have similar physical properties as FCC catalyst, including 
particle size distribution. Amongst other desirable features are large pores, high pore volume (> 
0.1 cm3/g) and surface area [8]. In this work, we prepared a passivator, Y-Mg-oxide, with structural 
properties given in Fig. 1. A high pore volume has been achieved; this reduces plugging effect of 
reaction with small pore materials, thereby, enhancing reaction with vanadium poison precursor.

3.2 
Metal deactivation of individual catalyst particles and influence of treatment 
 
temperature 
The matter whether vanadium poison is homogeneously distributed within the FCC catalyst 
components, or it is selectively deposited on a specific catalyst component has been investigated 
by Ruiz-Martínez et al. [4, 18]. In a related study, Buurmans et al. [19] investigated the catalytic 
activity of individual catalyst particle in the presence of contaminant metals. Therefore, to further 
widen knowledge on the dynamics of deactivation FCC catalyst in the presence of metals (e.g. 
vanadium), we studied the effect of laboratory treatment conditions on individual components of 
the FCC catalyst. The results presented in Fig. 2 reveals that the characteristic profiles of the zeolite 
(REY in the catalyst) within 2𝜃=5–35 °C are retained after hydrothermal treatment in 100% steam 
at 760 °C for 5 h in the absence of vanadium. Other components of the catalyst are severely 
deactivated at calcination temperatures. Particularly, kaolinite (clay) was completely deactivated 
at calcinations, agreeing with the report of Buurmans et al. [20]. During laboratory simulation of 
industrial E-cat according to the method of Mitchel [21], test catalysts for metal contamination 
studies are normally subjected to calcination in air between 500–600 °C, after oven drying. A 
catalyst sample was impregnated to 0.9 wt.% vanadium and subjected to heating at different 
temperatures and time. As shown in Fig. 3, the effect of temperature treatment preceding 
hydrothermal treatment in the laboratory simulation of catalyst deactivation was studied to realize 
vanadium mobility and to identify the vanadium species responsible for zeolite destruction. 
According to the figure, this treatment barely caused damage to the zeolite structure in the catalyst 
samples. Even at 760 °C calcination temperature, which the zeolite structure should have been 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

<<<PAGE 10>>>
severely affected, with the high vanadium content, since the melting point (690 °C) of vanadium 
(V) oxide is exceeded and vanadium was expected to be mobile, which can easily reach the 
framework of the zeolite, there is no remarkable degradation of crystallinity as indicated by 
retainment of the major reflections of Y-type zeolite [14]. The implication of this result is that 
vanadium oxide (V2O5) in which vanadium exists as in the catalyst under this condition is not the 
only operating species for the destruction of vanadium contaminated catalyst in the FCC unit.  
3.3. 
Structural analysis of passivated catalysts 
The crystalline structure of the catalysts was investigated by XRD analysis of the samples. Fig. 4 
shows patterns of catalysts with passiavtor, incorporated after impregnation of vanadium and 
drying in the oven, calcined in air at 550 °C for 6 h. The patterns reveal virtually sharp reflections 
which can be exclusively attributed to the zeolite phase. Vanadium passivated (with different 
dosages of passivator) and hydrothermally treated catalyst samples are compared with a sample 
impregnated with vanadium but had no passivator and treated accordingly (Fig. 5(I)). Evidently, 
Fig. 5(I)-a (without passivator) shows severe collapse of the zeolite structure, in accordance with 
behavior of unprotected catalyst containing vanadium impurities, after being subjected to 
hydrothermal treatment [8, 19]. Prominent peaks are those near 2𝜃 26.6 and 45.7°, corresponding 
to quartz (SiO2) and alumina (Al2O3) phases, respectively, a characteristic of amorphized sample. 
A careful dissection of all patterns revealed other peaks identified at ca. 23.8, 25.0 and 42.9° 2𝜃, 
and corresponding to zeolite (533 phase), YVO4 (JCPDS – 01-076-1649), and bulk MgO (JCPDS-
01-087-0651), respectively. Inspection of all the patterns reveals reduced 42.9° 2𝜃 peak for the 
samples (Fig. 5(I)-b&e), which may be due to the low concentration (3.0 wt.%) of the passivator 
in the sample (Fig. 5(I)-b) or a stronger interaction between the vanadium species and the 
passivator particles (bulk MgO) in the sample incorporated before vanadium impregnation (Fig.

5(I)-e). However, since our aim was to demonstrate the mechanism in which vanadium reaches 
the zeolite particle of the catalyst, where destruction originates, and the passivator particle as well, 
we chose to incorporate passivator after vanadium impregnation and drying for the experiments. 
Contrary to the report [8] that vanadium readily combines with magnesium to form magnesium

instead showed vanadate of yttrium (YVO4) which underscores the role of yttrium oxide in the

aged without calcination. However, this route resulted in higher bulk MgO in the resulting 
formulation. In any case, passivation is shown to have occurred but severity of the conditions 
seems influencing passivation effectiveness.

results support the insignificant impact of deactivation on these catalyst components by vanadium

Surface analysis of catalyst and passivator particles 
The catalyst/additive mixture was systematically analyzed on an SEM coupled with EDX, and the 
resulting images are shown in Fig. 12. Fig. 12a refers to a catalyst microsphere showing spots of 
smaller additive particles (white patches) on its surface. However, some isolated additive particles 
can be seen as irregular particles within the bulk mixture (Fig.12b). Fig. 12c shows an enlarged 
image of additive particles which has been confirmed from elemental scanning (Table 3). Point 
analysis revealed migration of vanadium from the catalyst particles to the passivator particle by a 
supposedly inter-particle transfer mechanism earlier reported [14, 22]. In Table 3, distribution of 
elements (EDX analysis) is shown. It can be observed that Al, Si and O have proximate 
concentrations and an even distribution at all locations (points 1 to 5). However, vanadium as well 
as additive elements (Mg and Y) were detected only at points that had high concentrations of 
passivator. It is worthy of note that for the passivator particles in the catalyst, the elemental 
composition from EDX would differ from that obtain for fresh passivator particle using XRF 
(results not shown). The reasons may be due to the fact that EDX is a semi-quantitative method 
that can only give the elemental content of a small domain (spot), and the composition of the 
element is calculated base on the total elemental composition, including Al, Si and V. To further 
verify vanadium migration, elemental scanning was also performed on supposed additive particles

3.6 
Evaluation of catalyst 
A gas oil feedstock was catalytically cracked on a micro activity (MAT) reactor to test the 
performance of metallated catalysts containing our prepared passivator. The test results (average 
of two runs) presented in Table 5, demonstrate effects of additive on cracking performance and 
product distribution. MAT catalyst activity ranges from 54.8 to 80.6 wt.%, with the pre-passivated 
and metallated (containing *7.5 wt.% Y-Mg-oxide) catalyst giving the highest activity (Fig. 13a). 
The insert in Fig. 13b shows the activity of the sample at two different temperatures. The 
performance of the sample containing 10 wt.% additive is illustrated at different catalyst-to-oil 
ratios (3.0-5.0). As would be expected, conversion increases with an increase in catalyst-to-oil 
ratio. This might be due to increase in effective active surface and acids sites of the catalyst for 
reaction with the feed molecules. Table 5 also compares normalized evaluation results of catalysts 
with different amount of the additive to that without additive. As can be seen, conversion increases 
as the additive dosage increases and reaches a maximum with 7.5 wt.% additive content at similar 
catalyst-to-oil ratio. The same trend is also shown for gasoline yield; however, LPG and coke 
content display different trends. Whereas, the highest gasoline yield was obtained using a catalyst 
containing 7.5 wt.% additive incorporated before metallation (*7.5 wt.% Y-Mg-oxide), it required 
a higher catalyst-to-oil ratio for the same gasoline proportion with a 10 wt.% additive catalyst 
sample. These results imply that an optimum yield of gasoline is achievable with 7.5 wt.% additive

3.7 
Mechanism of passivation 
The stability of a vanadium contaminated catalyst at 760 °C calcination temperature and the 
migration of vanadium from the catalyst particle to the passivator particle are clear indications that 
catalyst deactivation by vanadium occurs in the presence of a vanadium poison precursor, vanadic 
acid [8], which is formed in the regenerator during catalyst regeneration in the commercial units 
as mentioned earlier according to Eq.1. Because vanadic acid is a strong acid, (PKa = 0.05) [23], it 
shows the tendency of reaction with a base/basic material. As noted above, our passivator 
comprises two basic oxides (MgO and Y2O3). In theory, MgO is a stronger basic oxide than Y2O3; 
however, it lacks active solid catalyst acid center (Lewis acid). Tamura et al. [24] revealed that 
Y2O3 is a stronger solid acid catalyst (owing to presence of Lewis acid site) compared to MgO. 
This fact is also confirmed in our present study (Fig. 15). Mobile vanadic acid preferentially reacts 
with Y2O3 in bulk Mg-Y-oxide due to its higher acidity (active site) forming immobile vanadium 
compound (yttrium vanadate – YVO4). Formation of YVO4 can partially consume vanadic acid, 
making it less available to react with catalyst and MgO, and consequently less magnesium

vanadate formed, and less attack on the catalyst framework results. The formation of immobile 
YVO4 is revealed by XRD (Fig. 6). The overall mechanism appears, therefore, to be controlled by 
this metal oxide activity according to the reaction.

4.0 
Conclusion 
 
The deactivation and passivation mechanisms of vanadium contaminated FCC catalysts 
have been studied. A mixed metal oxide comprising magnesium and yttrium was demonstrated as 
an effective vanadium passivator during a FCC process. The passivation mechanism of the oxide 
is based on the acid-base chemistry principle. The results reveal that the passivator protects the 
catalyst by formation of immobile vanadate of yttrium with mobile vanadic acid, which reduces 
hydrolysis of the catalyst framework and thus, protects important properties of the catalyst. 
Catalyst samples with additive show quantifiable enhanced activity with the amount of additive, 
which collaborate physicochemical properties. Nevertheless, incorporating passivator before 
metallation gives enhanced yield of desirable products. These results can help open further studies 
of metal oxide activities which could be probable as vanadium passivator for FCC catalyst.
