A B S T R A C T  
To address climate change and environmental concerns, the chemical process industries are increasingly 
adopting green technology and exploring eco-friendly waste management practices. Deep Eutectic Solvents 
(DES) have emerged as promising green solvents with diverse applications in the industries in separation pro­
cesses, catalysis, drug design, and a lot more. However, the ideal way for modeling ionic interactions within the 
hydrogen-bond acceptor (HBA) during DES synthesis remains uncertain, and theoretical insight on the formation 
mechanism of energetically stable DESs from choline chloride (CHL) and urea (URE) is yet to be explored. In this 
study, we investigate different modeling approaches (no bond, generic bond, or single bond) for ionic in­
teractions in hydrogen-bond acceptor (HBA) molecules during DES synthesis and the thermodynamic feasibility 
of forming an energetically stable DES using choline chloride (CHL) and urea (URE) through theoretical cal­
culations (PM3, HF, and DFT). Our findings indicate that the choice of modeling approach does not significantly 
affect the outcomes across calculation levels. The H-X DES formation pathway emerges as the most viable route 
for CHL: GLC DES synthesis, offering strong binding energy and high exothermicity. This pathway provides a 
robust solution for synthesizing energetically stable DES with potential applications in various industries. Re­
searchers can consider alternative calculation levels when DFT becomes cost-prohibitive to optimize computa­
tional costs. Our study contributes valuable insights into DES design and synthesis, promoting environmentally 
sustainable and cost-effective processes.   
Introduction 
As the promotion of green technology continues to gain significant 
attention with the rising climatic change and global warming, the effort 
towards the improvement of pollution control and the promotion of 
environmental friendliness of the different operations in the oil and gas 
sector is likewise advancing [11,64]. Several areas promoting green 
technology and environmental friendliness in our respective commu­
nities are primarily explored in areas of waste management involving 
the transformation of wastes like agro-wastes, polymeric, and many 
other kinds of wastes into valuable products like fuels, materials, energy, 
and many other applications [16,17,38,47,48,57]. Recent studies are 
exploring the potential of some green solvents as potential solvents that 
could facilitate the extraction of certain materials of interest from a 
mixture. The study of the green solvents’ design and synthesis tech­
niques is significantly gaining better attention across different applica­
tions in process industries. Some of these processes include lactic acid 
production [43], gas absorption [24,34,58,65,68], sorption of some 
heavy metals and dyes [7,22,52,59], petroleum processing, enhanced oil 
recovery process [20,39], catalysis of chemical processes [61], recycling 
of wastes lithium batteries [15,33], and a lot more. Examples of such 
kinds of solvents include the deep eutectic solvent (DES) and ionic liquid 
(IL) [9,31,35]. However, this report focuses on DES exploration. For the 
purpose of this report, we focus on the exploration of DES’s potential for 
varying applications. Some important features of deep eutectic solvent 
(DES) include less toxicity, high degradability, good biocompatibility,

non-volatile, non-flammable, good renewability, and many other char­
acteristics [10]. The application of deep eutectic solvent (DES) is not 
limited to only separation process but also to catalysis, extraction, 
electrolytic process, and other processes, which could be applicable to a 
wide range of process industries like the food, pharmaceutical, cos­
metics, oil, and gas, and a lot more [5,27,32,60,66]. 
Some reports have categorized both of the solvents as a green sol­
vent, while other references like Chen and Mu [9] revisited the subject of 
the greenness of the solvent. Their report [9] has indicated that more 
and more evidence in the literature is showing the non-greenness of the 
solvents due to some characteristics exhibited by some of them, which 
include instability, volatility, toxicity, flammability, and difficulty in 
regenerating them for further use. Chen and Mu [9] further suggest the 
functionalization and modification of the solvent properties to reform 
their behavior to improve their greenness. Many works in the literature 
[5,10,27,32,34,59,60,66] have been exploring the suitability of the 
different forms of DES applications in the different processes. Some of 
which includes searches focusing on the identification of the stable DES 
for a potential application. In such a study, a wide range of studies do 
focus on the screening of hydrogen-bond acceptor (i.e., HBA) and 
hydrogen-bond donor (i.e., HBD) that would feasibly design a solvent 
with a defined characteristic of interest for whatever applications. Ex­
amples of some the HBD components include urea, glycerol, ethylene 
glycol, glucose, thiourea, acetamide, malonic acid, phenol, citric acid, 
maleic acid, succinic acid, and a lot more; and the HBA entails choline 
chloride, ethylammonium chloride, methyltriphenylphosphonium bro­
mide, betaine, 1,3-butanediol, acetylcholine chloride, choline tetra­
fluoroborate, triethylmethylammonium chloride, levulinic acid, choline 
proline and many others, which are used in the formation DES [2,44]. 
Some of the existing studies have synthesized DES for the sorption of 
certain pollutants like heavy metals, VOC, and dye [7,22,27,35,52]; the 
catalysis of some chemical processes [61]; and the promotion of sepa­
ration process efficiency. Many of these studies reported in the literature 
have primarily deployed experimental study approaches, while only a 
few studies have employed a theoretical approach to investigate its 
synthesis process using a computational approach. Among the few 
theoretical studies is the work of Naseem et al. [41] who studied the 
relationship between computed IR spectra for choline chloride-based 
deep eutectic solvents with ones experimentally collected. From 
which, the study confirms a good agreement with the experiment one. 
Ghenaarian et al. [21] studies further established that choline chloride: 
urea better interacts with palladium nanoparticles than the choline 
chloride (CHL): ethylene glycol (EGL) deep eutectic solvent (DES). Using 
a theoretical approach, the use of guanidine hydrochloride (GuHCl): 
lactic acid (LA) in the ratio of 1:1 has been established to have formed 
DES that could dissolve hemicellulose due to the higher interaction 
deduced for it from their studies of lactic acid-based DES [67]. Doherty 
and Acevedo [14] developed a reliable non-polarizable force field 
(OPLS-DES) for effective prediction of DES’ density, heat capacity, 
surface tension, and viscosity, which shows a good agreement with the, 
experiment. Nicolas et al. [19] also explores the role of DES in enhancing 
the solubility of desired component present in a mixture during 
extraction processes. The thermophysical and transport properties were 
also investigated by Cea-Klapp et al. [8] for the DESs like CHL:EGL and 
CHL:GLC in presence of methanol using molecular dynamics, while 
some work [1] investigated the impact of water and HBA on the density 
and viscosity of a DES for providing insight on the design and re-design 
of new solvent. Other works like Santra et al. [53] were theoretically 
able to have a stable geometry of the choline chloride (CHL): urea (URE) 
deep eutectic solvent (DES) as a measure for understanding the stability 
of DES formed from the use of HBD (URE) and HBA (CHL). 
The existing literature review has not yet provided a definitive 
approach for modeling the ionic interaction within the hydrogen-bond 
acceptor (HBA) building. It remains unclear whether this interaction 
should be represented with no bonds, generic bonds, or single bonds. It 
is imperative to clearly establish whether any of these modeling 
approaches can influence the outcomes of a study. 
Furthermore, the computational investigation of the mechanism 
involved in synthesizing a highly stable choline chloride (CHL): glycerol 
(GL) deep eutectic solvent (DES) has not been comprehensively explored 
in the current body of literature. DESs created using polyol-based 
hydrogen bond donors (HBDs) have gained widespread industrial ap­
plications, particularly those composed of glycerol and ethylene glycol, 
owing to their lower freezing points and ability to remain in liquid form 
below room temperature [12,18,26]. 
To address the existing research limitations stated earlier in our 
survey of the literature, we, therefore, explore the different possible 
approaches to modeling the electrovalent or ionic interaction existing in 
the hydrogen-bond acceptor (HBA) molecule used in the synthesis of 
deep eutectic solvent (DES) to understand the agreement and the 
implication of using either of the approaches (using no bond, generic 
bond, or single bond) in a study. Furthermore, we investigate the 
mechanism involved with forming an energetically stable DES from the 
use of choline chloride (CHL) and glycerol (GLC). In addition, we later 
investigated the prediction agreement that existed at different levels of 
calculations like PM3, Hartree Fock (HF), and density functional theory 
(DFT). Findings made from this study serve as an established strategy for 
building different stable DES(s) for screening out solvents that best suit a 
defined application or use to be investigated at a low and high level of 
computational cost. 
Methodology 
In the study, we employed a computational approach via a quantum 
mechanics calculation. The later section of this report presents 
comprehensive details on the strategy and computational information 
deployed in our study. 
Study strategy 
In this section, we present the strategies employed in the evaluation 
of our chosen computational approach strengths and limitations, the 
assessment of the approaches used in the modeling of ionic bonds, and 
one used for the identification of feasible pathways leading to the for­
mation of the deep eutectic solvent and its corresponding formation 
energy. 
Evaluation of the des models’ components used in our study 
We employed an infrared spectroscopy plot computationally gener­
ated for the hydrogen bond acceptor and hydrogen bond donor to 
compare with one experimentally obtained from a wet laboratory. We 
evaluated the similarities and differences visible on their respective plot 
or spectra. The computationally generated spectra or plot is obtained for 
different levels of computations and compared with experimentally 
generated ones to understand the strength and limitations of the 
different levels of computations evaluated. 
Evaluation of different ionic bond modeling techniques in HBA 
With the use of semi-empirical, Hartree Fock, and density functional 
theory calculations, different approaches to modeling the ionic bonds 
present in HBA were evaluated. The approaches, which include 
modeling the ionic interaction without using no bond (that is, space), 
with the use of single bond, and generic bonds (that is, a type of bond 
which does not physically distinct bond formation or breaking), were all 
evaluated as shown in Fig. 1. 
All the sets of approaches were evaluated at the different levels of 
computation (semi-empirical, Hartree Fock, and density functional 
theory calculations [51]), assessing their electronic energies and ionic 
bond length consistency across the three approaches evaluated. Choline 
chloride was taken as our HBA component for our deep eutectic solvent.

Evaluation of the DES formation mechanism 
The study evaluated the different deep eutectic solvent formation 
mechanisms by analyzing different interaction points on HBA (Cl, H, O, 
and N) with HBD (O, H, and C) with their corresponding formation re­
action energy, also known as binding energy. The energies are computed 
using the expression in Eq. (1).  
BE = Edes - EHBA – EHBD                                                            
(1) 
The Edes is the electronic energies of the DES, EHBA is the electronic 
energies of the hydrogen bond acceptor, and EHBD is the electronic 
energies of the hydrogen donor. All the electronic energies are collected 
in eV. The energy that is most exothermic, shown with the highest 
negative value, would signify the best and most feasible interaction 
points. 
In our study, we calculated both uncorrected and BSSE-corrected 
binding energies to comprehensively assess their differences and er­
rors. We have chosen to utilize the corrected binding energies (referred 
to as BEcorr) for our subsequent analysis concerning the formation route 
stability of Deep Eutectic Solvents (DES). Detailed information 
regarding the BSSE correction methodology employed in our energy 
calculations to derive BEcorr is provided in Section 2.2 under ’Compu­
tational Details’. 
Thermodynamic feasibility of the different DES formation pathways 
The various thermodynamic parameters such as enthalpy, entropy, 
and Gibbs free energy were all computed use the generic mathematical 
expression in Eq. (2).  
dX = Xdes – XHBA – XHBD                                                           
(2) 
The subject dX denotes the formation X, where the X could be either 
H, S or G like dH, dS or dG. Xdes, XHBA, and XHBD terms represent the X 
values for the DES formed, HBA, and HBD compound. The enthalpy (H), 
entropy (S), and Gibbs free energy (G) are all computed standard tem­
perature (298 K) and pressure (1 atm). 
Computational details 
Using the Spartan v20 molecular modeling package, we employed 
three different levels of computations in our study. The levels including 
semi-empirical calculations, which we utilized to compute infrared 
spectra, electronic energies, and other relevant properties used in the 
methods comparison was PM3, Hartree Fock calculation was also 
employed using 6–31G* basis set in the calculations of infrared spectra, 
electronic energies, and other properties. Another level of calculations 
we employed in our study is density functional theory calculations using 
B3LYP hybrid functional and 6–31 G in calculating infrared spectra, 
electronic energies, and other relevant parameters, in accordance with 
literature [25]. A Dell Precision 3520 mobile workstation with RAM of 
24 GB, a processor capacity of 7 cores, and a storage of 1 TB SSD was 
employed to run our calculations. 
To improve on the accuracy of the energies obtained from the ge­
ometry optimization calculations by correcting the basis set super­
position error (BSSE), we employed the use of single-point energy 
calculation using DFT-B3LYP hybrid function and dual basis set (cc- 
pVTZ [6–31G*], via a combination a correlated corrected basis set [cc- 
pVTZ] and pople double-eta basis set [6–31G*]) to re-evaluate our 
binding energies in line with the literature [29,63], which suggests the 
use of dual basis set as one of measure for correcting the BSSE in our 
package. The outcome of employing this approach was designated as the 
BSSE-corrected binding energy (BEcorr), whereas binding energies ob­
tained without its application were labeled as uncorrected binding en­
ergies (BEncorr).

Analysis of the CHL-GLC deep eutectic solvent stability 
In this section, we evaluate the stability of the DES formed by choline 
chloride (HBA) and glycerol (HBD) by probing the interatomic inter­
action of the atoms in the HBA (Cl-, H, N+, and O atom) with the HBD 
and also the HBA and HBD in their unbonded state during their struc­
tural formation. CHL and GLC, the HBA and HBD component of the DES, 
whose energetically stable formation mechanism explored in this report 
shows −21,472.65 and −9381.60 eV electronic energy, respectively. 
Exploration of DES formation via the Cl-X 
The analysis of the DES formation from CHL and GLC was evaluated 
for possible formation via the different interaction routes Cl-X (X = HO, 
OH, CH, HC, CH2, H2C) involving the Cl- in CHL interacting with other 
components like O, H, and C present in the GLC (the HBD). In this 
analysis, several interactions evaluated include OH–Cl-HO (the inter­
action of Cl- with two H-atoms of two different O–H functions in GLC), 
HO–Cl-OH (the interaction of Cl- with two O-atoms from two O–H 
functions of GLC), CH–Cl-HO (that is, the interaction of Cl- with two H- 
atoms from C–H and O–H functions of GLC), Cl-OH (the interaction of 
Cl- with one O-atom in O–H functions of GLC), Cl-CH2 (the interaction 
of Cl- with one C-atoms from H–C-H function of GLC), Cl-HC (the 
interaction of Cl- with one H-atom from C–H function of GLC), and 
physisorption without any constraint of a chemical bond in the model. 
Findings from the study of the aforementioned interaction via the 
chloride ion have confirmed our study to have consistently restructured 
into a new geometrical structure, which favors the interaction of Cl- with 
H-atom on O–H functions. The study reveals that some of the optimized 
structures for the DES formed via the various interaction routes inves­
tigated show a single, and some show multiple bonds with the different 
O–H functions present in GLC. Results shown in Table 7 are presented 
in the order of their interaction strength, where OH–Cl-HO was found 
to have shown much stronger interaction compared to other interactions 
investigated in the study, as seen from its binding energy (BE) value. 
Fig. 7 presents the geometrical structure of the DES for the optimized or 
relaxed structure and the unrelaxed structure (that is, the structure 
before the geometrical optimization) of the best interaction (OH–Cl- 
HO) obtained for the investigation of Cl-X (X = HO, OH, CH, HC, CH2, 
H2C). Fig. 7 shows that the OH–Cl-HO bond lengths were shorter before 
optimization than after being optimized. The increase in the bond length 
of some interactions with bond lengths greater than 3 Å confirms them 
as weak interactions, contrary to other interactions that are stronger due 
to the shorter bond length obtained for the atom pairs, following the 
report of Pauling [49] that established the dependency of bond energy 
on its bond length. The strong interactions with bond lengths of around 
the range of 1.8 to 3 Å [13,30] were confirmed to be hydrogen bonding, 
while the weaker ones with bond lengths greater than 3 Å [40] were 
identified to be Van de Waal interaction, following the related reports 
[13,30,40] in the literature. 
Further analysis of the bond length reports in Table 7 shows the 
strength of the OH–Cl-HO interaction, which was evident in its bond 
length obtained for its interaction with OH21-Cl1 and OH12-Cl1, which 
were found to be relatively low compared to other O–H like OH8 and 
OH1 that were longer. The bond length implies that there is a weak 
interaction of Cl with H8 and H1 compared to H21 and H12, which were 
stronger, in agreement with the literature [49] that established the de­
pendency of bond energy on its bond length. A close range of shorter 
bond lengths was also found for the case of HO–Cl-OH and CH–Cl-HO, 
which also show a strong interaction with H21 and H12, unlike the case 
of other H-atoms (H8 and H1) in other O–H functions. Other in­
teractions like Cl-OH, Cl-CH2, and Cl-HC that were investigated were 
found to have shown much weaker interaction for all the H-atoms except 
for the one of the H-atom, which offers a better interaction due to its 
shorter bond length relatively found in other H-atoms of the alcohol 
function. So, in the evaluation of the Cl-X (X = HO, OH, CH, HC, CH2, 
H2C), the OH–Cl-HO shows the best interaction with a stronger binding 
energy of BEcorr = −0.49 eV and shorter bond length for Cl-atom with 
two other H-atoms of the alcohol (OH) functions in GLC, according to 
the literature [49].

Thermodynamic analysis of the most stable DES formation pathways 
The resulting outcome of the various pathways that could lead to the 
formation of the CHL:GLC deep eutectic solvent reported in Ta­
bles 7,8,9,10, where the most feasible path across each of the routes for 
Cl-X, H-X, N-X, and O-X were identified using their binding energies (BE) 
was employed in the thermodynamic analysis of the paths. Table 11 
summarizes the best paths across each route studied and their corre­
sponding outcome from the thermodynamic analysis carried out for the 
routes. 
The results show that all the formation pathways interact with two 
H-atoms except for the H-X and N-X pathways that interact with three H- 
atoms, which showed higher binding strength for H-X and N-X, 
excluding the case of physisorption. The analysis further reveals that all 
the formation routes are spontaneous and exothermic due to the nega­
tive entropy and enthalpy changes obtained for all the routes, in 
accordance with the literature [6,23,37,55]. However, the trend of their 
degree of disorderliness and exothermicity was in the order of H-X 〈N-X 
< Phys < O-X < Cl-X and H-X 〉Phys > N-X > O-X > Cl-X, confirming 
DES form via H-X to be the most exothermic and spontaneous formation 
pathway compared other pathways. With the use of the Gibbs formation 
energies, the exergonicity of the formation pathways was found to have 
followed the trend reported for the formation enthalpies in Table 11, in 
accordance with the literature [6,23,37,55]. The study confirms the H-X 
DES formation pathway, which involves the interaction of three H-atoms 
present in the alcohol functions of the HBD molecule (GLC), showing the 
strongest binding energy (or formation energy), most exothermic, 
spontaneous, and exergonic pathway leading to the formation of DES 
from CHL and GCL for any potential applications, in agreement with the 
literature [4,46,53,62], which shows that report the most negative 
binding energy to be the strongest interaction in a study. 
This study’s approach yielded more effective and straightforward 
findings compared to using maps for HOMO-LUMO energies and elec­

mation of a stable deep eutectic solvent (CHL:GLC) raised certain 
limitations.

GLC) and HBA (Cl on CHL) molecules, as indicated by these maps, did

Conclusions and recommendations 
Isn this study, we successfully investigated the implications of using 
different approaches (using no bond, generic bond, or single bond) to 
model the electrovalent or ionic interactions present in a hydrogen-bond 
acceptor (HBA) molecule used in the synthesis of deep eutectic solvent 
(DES). The study identified a thermodynamically feasible pathway to an 
energetically stable DES using choline chloride (CHL) and urea (URE). 
Additionally, we thoroughly assessed the suitability of employing 
different levels of theoretical calculations like PM3, Hartree Fock (HF), 
and density functional theory (DFT). 
Our study revealed that the use of no bond, generic, or single bond 
for modeling the ionic or electrovalent bonds in HBA molecules did not 
show any significant difference across the levels (PM3, HF, and DFT) of 
calculation deployed in our studies. Therefore, any of these approaches 
can be used in modeling with freedom; however, we recommend 
maintaining consistency by using only one approach in a particular 
study. 
The findings from our study demonstrate that the H-X DES formation 
pathway, involving the interaction of three H-atoms present in the 
alcohol functions of the hydrogen bond donor (HBD) molecule (glycerol 
- GLC) with the chloride ion in the HBA molecule (choline chloride - 
CHL), has been identified as the thermodynamically feasible path for the 
CHL:GLC DES formation. The H-X pathway exhibited the most
