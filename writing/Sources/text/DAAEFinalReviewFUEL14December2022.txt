Abstract  
9 
The workhorse residue fluid catalytic cracking catalyst used to process heavy feedstocks, 
10 
such as atmospheric residue, has good catalytic activity, selectivity, hydrothermal stability, 
11 
fluidability, and coke selectivity. Processing these heavy feedstocks has several challenges 
12 
due to heteroatoms such as vanadium, nickel, sodium, iron, sulfur, and nitrogen. To give 
13 
perspective on these challenges, motivation for and recent technologies for Residue Fluid 
14 
Catalytic Cracking (RFCC) have been reviewed. Also highlighted are the relevance of (i) 
15 
multi-zone coordinated-controlled, (ii) two-stage riser (conventional), (iii) two-stage riser (for 
16 
propylene production), and (iv) auxiliary riser (gasoline modification) on reactor 
17 
performances. These heteroatoms are detrimental to the catalysts as they act to either 
18 
permanently or temporarily deactivate them, thereby promoting undesirable reactions such as 
19 
dehydrogenation and coke production. Furthermore, the impact of the catalytic cracking 
20 
catalyst selectivities on low molecular weight production, especially propylene and other 
21 
olefins, has been pointed out. It is, therefore, important to develop a catalyst with improved 
22 
metal tolerance while maintaining its intrinsic properties. Antimony, bismuth, boron, 
23 
magnesium, tin, and rare earth compound (cerium, lanthanum) have attracted attention 
24 
recently due to their abilities to trap and passivate Ni, V, Na, and Fe against their harmful 
25 
effects on the catalyst.

Introduction 
29 
Fluid catalytic cracking (FCC) is a secondary conversion process in the petroleum 
30 
refining industry used to convert high-molecular-weight, high-boiling fractions of petroleum 
31 
to valuable fuels such as gasoline, diesel, olefinic gases, and liquefied petroleum gas (LPG). 
32 
Globally, 59 countries have active refineries with FCC and a total refinery FCC unit capacity 
33 
of 14,259 mbd in 2020 [1]. The biggest refineries in the world in terms of the refinery FCC 
34 
unit capacity in 2020 include Baton Rough (US), Baytown (US), Jamnagar I (India), 
35 
Jamnagar II (India), and Texas City III (US) [1]. Some of the largest upcoming refineries 
36 
with FCC units in the world during the outlook period of 2021-2025 include the Dangote 
37 
refinery, Lagos (Nigeria), Yulong (China), and Turban II (Indonesia) [2]. 
38 
The FCC unit is regarded as the most important conversion unit and the cash cow of 
39 
the refinery because the fuel produced from this unit alone accounts for 35 to 50% of the total 
40 
gasoline produced globally in the petroleum industry [3–6]. However, the world crude oil 
41 
supply changes towards heavier crudes, coupled with the quest by refiners to process cheaper 
42 
crude to increase refinery margins, and the increasing demand for propylene and 
43 
transportation fuel, have led to the development of the residue fluid catalytic (RFCC) process 
44 
to convert residue feedstocks. A shared setback established in both the FCC and RFCC units 
45 
is the regular decrease of catalytic performance caused by the gradual deposition of 
46 
contaminant metals such as Ni, V, Fe, and Na on the catalyst during the processing of heavy 
47 
hydrocarbon feedstocks [7–12]. These metals destroy the catalyst and promote undesirable 
48 
side reactions, such as producing large amounts of coke and hydrogen. A large quantity of 
49 
hydrogen is undesirable because it affects downstream equipment such as wet gas 
50 
compressors (WGC). The WGC is volume-limited and not designed to handle gases beyond a 
51 
certain volume. Production of a large amount of coke upsets the reactor and regenerator heat 
52 

<<<PAGE 3>>>
 3
balance as well as affects the main air blower (MAB), which like WGC, is also limited in the 
53 
amount of air it takes to burn off the coke in the regenerator during the regeneration process 
54 
and leads to a reduction in feed throughput. In addition to upsetting the heat balance and the 
55 
MAB, a large amount of coke temporarily deactivates the RFCC catalyst by blocking the 
56 
active sites, thereby reducing the catalyst activity. A way to possibly overcome such a 
57 
setback is by deploying environmentally friendly passivating agents in the RFCC catalyst 
58 
mix.  
59 
Over the years, several authors have published reviews on the conventional FCC unit, 
60 
including processes, catalyst materials, poisons, and deactivation mechanisms. 
61 
Vogt and Weckhuysen, [13] reviewed the developments of FCC, the so-called grand old lady 
62 
of zeolite catalysis. The study discussed the general background of the FCC process, 
63 
including a short history, process description, chemical reactions, reactor design, and catalyst 
64 
material. The study also evaluated the means to improve the stability of zeolite structure, 
65 
propylene selectivity, overall catalyst accessibility, and a state-of-the-art micro-spectroscopy 
66 
technique for characterising FCC catalysts at the single particle. The findings, however, 
67 
suggested that shifts in the availability of feedstocks, including tight oils, and renewables, 
68 
coupled with longer-term demand for gasoline, middle distillates, and propylene, require 
69 
further developments in both catalysts and process. 
70 
Bai et al. [14] reviewed the contamination of fluid catalytic cracking catalysts 
71 
describing the status of FCC technology, such as catalysts, the variation in feedstocks, and 
72 
the effects of contaminants of the FCC catalysts. The deposition mechanisms of multiple 
73 
impurities on cracking catalysts during the FCC process, including nitrogen, sulfur, metals, 
74 
and coke originating from the feedstock during the FCC reaction, were also discussed. It was 
75 
reported that vanadium, nickel, iron, and sodium are the metal contaminants having the most 
76 
harmful effects that can significantly impact the catalyst structure and performance. It was 
77 

<<<PAGE 4>>>
 4
also found that nitrogen and sulfur are considered less harmful compared to metal 
78 
contaminants. Pore blockage by the coking effect of sulfur and acid site neutralization by 
79 
nitrogen are serious problems too. 
80 
A review of the deactivation of FCC catalysts by cyclic propylene steaming (CPS) 
81 
presented some modifications made to the CPS method to further decrease the destructive 
82 
effects of vanadium on FCC catalysts during deactivation [15]. The methods comprise (i) 
83 
adjusting the ratio of the time the catalyst spends in reducing and oxidising atmospheres 
84 
during the CPS cycles and (ii) exposing the metalated catalyst to pre-stabilisation steps with 
85 
reduction-oxidation cycles. Comparing the activities and selectivity data of FCC catalysts 
86 
with (a) age distribution and (b) CPS revealed that both have similar catalyst rankings. 
87 
Moreover, reduction-oxidation cycles were found to simulate the deactivation of FCC 
88 
catalysts in the absence of contaminant metals better than traditional deactivation in a 100% 
89 
steam atmosphere.  
90 
FCC catalyst deactivation was also reviewed by O’Connor and Pouwels [16] with a 
91 
focus on the complexity of the inter-relations in catalyst deactivation by (i) ageing, (ii) 
92 
poisoning and fouling, (iii) the effect on heat balance and catalyst circulation rate, and (iv) 
93 
the quantification and modelling of FCC catalyst deactivation. The findings suggest that for 
94 
catalyst poisoning at high metal contents and hydrothermal ageing with low metals, cyclic 
95 
deactivation of the catalyst in numerous age fractions is the preferred route for a realistic 
96 
simulation and quantification of the deactivation phenomena. 
97 
Patrick-Walker and Peterman [17] reviewed how the contemporary residue FCC 
98 
(RFCC) units set a new standard for propylene production by exploring the technical 
99 
challenges and opportunities in targeting high propylene production from RFCC units. The 
100 
study summarised the advances in RFCC operations and reliability. The differences between 
101 
RFCC operations and conventional FCC based on the catalysts, feed properties, operating 
102 

<<<PAGE 5>>>
 5
parameters, and equipment design were also evaluated. The authors pointed out that the 
103 
processing of heavily contaminated residue using the Universal Oil Products (UOP) 
104 
combustor type regenerators with catalyst coolers is gaining wider acceptance alongside two-
105 
stage regenerators with catalyst coolers due to their ability to withstand the high temperature 
106 
that results from high Conradson carbon residues present in the residue feedstock. 
107 
In an attempt to discuss the catalyst structure suitable for the large molecular weight 
108 
feed to the RFCC unit, Ishihara [18] reviewed advances in the preparations and reactivity of 
109 
hierarchical zeolite catalysts for the catalytic cracking of carbonaceous materials. The 
110 
carbonaceous materials include simple straight-chained and substituted-aromatic 
111 
hydrocarbons, heavy molecules in mixtures such as vacuum gas oil (VGO), atmospheric 
112 
residue, and biomass like vegetable oil. The study explained the preparation of hierarchical 
113 
zeolite catalysts in detail and described how the catalyst matrix pre-cracks the large 
114 
molecular weight hydrocarbon feed before it gets into the highly active micropores of the 
115 
zeolite for selective cracking into the desired products such as gasoline and olefins. It was 
116 
found that hierarchical catalysts with both microporous and mesoporous structures exhibited 
117 
a better performance due to the increase in the accessibility and the diffusivity in mesoporous 
118 
structure and the high catalytic activity in the microporous zeolite. 
119 
Previous review studies focused on the catalytic cracking and catalyst deactivation of 
120 
mainly the conventional FCC processes and catalyst poisons. While some of the reviews have 
121 
identified the various catalyst poisons and their passivators, to the best of our knowledge, no 
122 
work in the open literature adequately reviewed the new developments in the use of effective 
123 
and environmentally friendly passivator. Nickel and vanadium have been identified as the 
124 
most detrimental poisons of RFCC catalysts as they promote competing reactions such as 
125 
dehydrogenation and coke formation. Antimony has been used to mitigate the 
126 

<<<PAGE 6>>>
 6
dehydrogenating effect of nickel. However, it has been found that antimony causes emission 
127 
issues and is unsafe to handle. 
128 
For the first time, this review presents an overview of the current RFCC processes and 
129 
the various passivators and traps inhibiting the harmful effects of the metals and non-metal 
130 
contaminants on RFCC catalysts. The advances in the passivation of RFCC catalysts against 
131 
(i) nickel contaminants: using antimony, rare earth metals, bismuth, and boron as 
132 
passivators/traps, and those (ii) vanadium contaminants: using tin, rare earth, and basic 
133 
alkaline compound as passivators/traps were discussed. The associated challenges and future 
134 
research and development based on (i) design of RFCC catalyst, (ii) replacement of antimony 
135 
as nickel passivators, (iii) development of RFCC catalyst that incorporates boron as 
136 
environmentally friendly nickel passivator, and (iv) insight into the passivation of vanadium, 
137 
were highlighted.

Residue Fluid Catalytic Cracking (RFCC) is a variation of fluid catalytic cracking that 
158 
has been widely applied in recent years in an attempt to solve the problem of proliferation of 
159 
heavier and more dirty feedstocks to the conventional FCC unit as a result of the decrease in 
160 
the world’s supply of light and sweet crudes [24]. In this case, the feed stream to the process 
161 
is the high molecular weight bottom of the atmospheric distillation column, called an 
162 
atmospheric residue, with high Conradson carbon residue (CCR) and higher contaminant 
163 
content like metals, nitrogen, and sulfur. Due to the feed stream characteristics, the residue 
164 
catalytic cracking units require design and optimization changes. The higher levels of CCR in 
165 
the feed stream led to higher temperatures in the catalyst regeneration step and a lower 
166 
catalyst circulation rate to keep the reactor at a constant temperature. Reduction in the 
167 
catalyst/oil ratio, decreasing conversion, and selectivity are some of the adverse effects of 
168 
high CCR. The RFCC units, however, normally rely on catalyst coolers to avoid these effects 
169 
[21,24].

173 
molecular weight components, the conventional FCC cannot meet the performance target

regarding olefin yield, gasoline production, and lower coke deposition. The higher the 
175 
amount of high molecular weight compounds, the less the capacity of FCC to meet the

Improvements have been made to commercial FCC technologies since its inception in 
181 
1942, including changes in catalysts, engineering, and facilities. These improvements have 
182

183 
feedstocks [27]. The newly improved FCC technologies involve the modification of gasoline, 
184

2.1 
Multi-zone coordinated-controlled 
188 
The multi-zone coordinated-controlled (MZCC) RFCC technology (Fig. 1a) is designed 
189 
based on a high oil-to-catalyst mixing energy reaction to produce liquid and light petroleum 
190 
products. The primary benefit of the MZCC RFCC technology is that it increases the yield of 
191 
light oil and liquids and reduces the formation of coke and dry gas even when processing 
192 
heavy feedstocks. These benefits are high energy back-mixing for the feeding zone and high 
193 
effective contact for catalyst and oil. The major disadvantage of this technology is that it 
194 
leads to an increased level of nickel and iron [23]. These metals deposit on the catalyst and 
195 
promote unfavourable reactions such as coke formation and dehydrogenation [29]. Some of 
196 
the technical features of the MZCC RFCC technology [23,28] are: 
197 
• High effective contact for oil to the catalyst and high energy back-mixing for the 
198 
feeding zone 
199 
•  Rapid separation of oil and catalyst for the exit zone 
200 
• Chemical stripping for heavy components for the stripping zone 
201 
• 
Orderly proceeding and plug flow for the reaction zone

2.2 
 Two-stage riser (conventional) 
203 
The conventional Two-stage riser (Fig. 1b) technology comprises two separate riser 
204 
reactors that can efficiently hinder over-cracking, decrease coke yield and enhance the yield 
205 
and properties of light oil. The first reactor is for the fresh feedstock and the second reactor is 
206 
for the entry of cycle oil. The two reactors are in contact with high active regenerant. A two-
207 
stage riser removes unwanted product distribution due to an over-long riser and reaction 
208 
competition in the same reactor between fresh feedstock and cycle oil [30]. 
209 
The conventional two-stage riser RFCC technology is also designed to produce light 
210 
petroleum products. Its major advantages come from the short contact time between the 
211 
catalyst and the hydrocarbon vapour. The brief contact time helps to reduce over-cracking, 
212 
which reduces dry gas and coke formation. The major shortcoming of this technology is the 
213 
complexity of the process. Some of the technical features of a Two-stage riser (conventional) 
214 
[23,30] are: 
215 
• Staged reaction: determine reaction condition, and avert competition between cycle 
216 
oil and fresh feed 
217 
• Catalyst relay: improved overall activity due to two-path catalysts circulation, 
218 
preventing a thermal reaction, and increasing catalytic reaction 
219 
• Short reaction duration: lower over-cracking of the target product, decreases coke 
220 
yield, improves light oil yield, and enhances product distribution 
221 
• High catalyst-to-oil ratio: effective increase of the catalyst-to-oil ratio and formation 
222 
of a new thermal balance due to two path circulation of catalyst 
223

2.5 
Limitations of FCC and the development of RFCC technologies 
280 
The fluid catalytic cracking (FCC) process is regarded as the most important refinery 
281 
process due to its ability to convert the heavy portion of the barrel of crude into more useful 
282 
petroleum products. In recent times, there has been a quest by refiners to process cheaper 
283 
crudes. More so, coupled with the decline in the production of light and sweet crudes, the 
284 
feed to the conventional FCC unit has become heavy and dirty.  
285 
Processing heavier feedstocks poses many challenges to the conventional FCC design 
286 
due to the heavy metal poisons and coke laydown on the catalyst during the cracking 
287 
reactions. Metal poisons such as nickel, vanadium, iron and sodium deposit on the cracking 
288 
catalyst and promote a dehydrogenation reaction, thereby shifting the selectivity of the 
289 
catalyst from the intended products. In addition to fostering undesirable reactions, the metal 
290 
poisons, especially vanadium, acts to destroy the catalyst structure leading to a loss in activity 
291 
of the catalyst. A large amount of Conradson carbon residue in the heavy feed leads to a large 
292 
amount of coke laydown on the catalyst, leading to a large amount of unwanted heat 
293 
generated during regeneration to restore the catalyst’s activity. 
294 

<<<PAGE 17>>>
 17
These changes cannot be resolved by simple design modifications or changes in the 
295 
process conditions. Therefore, RFCC technology was developed to address the shortcomings 
296 
of the conventional FCC process. RFCC operations vary from traditional FCC operations in 
297 
many significant ways, such as catalyst, operating conditions, feed characteristics, and 
298 
equipment design, as presented earlier in Table 1. The feed to the RFCC process is the 
299 
atmospheric residue (AR), the heaviest fraction in a barrel of crude oil and boils at the 
300 
maximum temperatures. AR contains large quantities of polycyclic aromatics with low API 
301 
gravity and Conradson carbon residue (CCR) greater than 4wt%. The catalyst for cracking 
302 
AR feedstock should have features beyond the conventional FCC catalyst, which utilizes 
303 
vacuum gas oil as feedstock. The catalyst structure needs to accommodate the large 
304 
polycyclic aromatic molecules in the AR. Such catalyst structure is achieved by incorporating 
305 
matrix components containing large volumes of pores or creating hierarchical catalyst 
306 
structures. The catalyst can also be formulated with passivators to take care of the metal 
307 
poisons present in the heavy residue feedstock. As part of the modification to the equipment 
308 
design, catalyst coolers are installed in the RFCC process to utilize the large amount of heat 
309 
generated due to high CCR to produce steam.

3 
RFCC Catalyst  
311 
A catalyst for cracking residue feedstock must have different features from the 
312 
catalyst for vacuum gas oil (VGO) feedstock due to the large polycyclic aromatic molecules 
313 
in residue feedstock. The uniform micropore distribution of zeolites is responsible for their 
314 
high shape selectivity observed in various reactions. However, this feature limits diffusion 
315 
when large molecules diffuse in relatively narrow channels. Some active sites are hindered 
316 
from contacting reactants due to restricted transport processes [35,36]. Consequently, 
317 
developing new materials with a hierarchical porosity architecture is highly needed. The 
318 
catalyst for the cracking of residue feedstock has to be designed to tolerate the structure of 
319 

<<<PAGE 18>>>
 18
the large molecules of residue feedstock because the conventional FCC zeolite catalyst has an 
320 
average pore diameter of 0.74 nm. 
321 
In comparison, the atmospheric residue that serves as feed to the modern RFCC 
322 
process has molecular constituents with diameters of 1.2 to 15 nm, making it impossible for 
323 
the large molecules to enter into the zeolite micropores to be cracked directly [37,38]. 
324 
Designing a catalyst to accommodate the large-diameter residue feedstock is typically 
325 
accomplished by incorporating a matrix component with a significant pore volume. Several 
326 
attempts have been made to create RFCC catalysts with large pore volumes by synthesising 
327 
hierarchical zeolite-Y catalysts using various methods. These methods include the bottom-up 
328 
and top-down approaches [39–44]. In addition, the catalyst should have a low delta (∆) coke 
329 
character to stand the high delta (∆) coke tendencies of the contaminated residue feedstock as 
330 
a result of the high amount of Conradson carbon residue (CCR), which is usually greater than 
331 
4 wt% in the feed [17]. The low delta coke character of the catalyst is accomplished through 
332 
specific catalyst design features and targeting an equilibrium catalyst activity more superior 
333 
than that associated with the VGO catalyst. Finally, the catalyst must tolerate the metals in 
334 
the feed, particularly vanadium and nickel, as these metals are deposited on the cracking 
335 
catalyst, destroying the catalyst structure and promoting undesirable reactions. These 
336 
unwanted reactions include the production of large amounts of H2 through dehydrogenation 
337 
and producing large quantities of coke. The large amounts of H2 are detrimental to the wet 
338 
gas compressor because it is volume limited. At the same time, a large amount of coke upsets 
339 
the reactor-regenerator heat balance and affects the main air blower, in addition to 
340 
temporarily deactivating the catalyst. 
341

3.1 
Catalyst for the Reduction of Olefins in Gasoline 
363 
The residue fluid catalytic cracking (RFCC) gasoline usually contains 20 – 40 wt% of 
364 
olefins [45–47]. The presence of olefins in RFCC gasoline is advantageous because it helps 
365 
to increase the gasoline octane number. However, the high content of olefinic compounds 
366 
affects the engine exhaust emissions and the stability of the fuel under storage conditions. 
367 
Olefins react with atmospheric oxygen leading to oxidation and polymerisation products, 
368 

<<<PAGE 21>>>
 21
forming gum in the gasoline tank [48,49]. According to European Standard EN228, the olefin 
369 
content in gasoline is currently limited to 18 vol% [50]. 
370 
Several attempts have been made to reduce olefins by hydrogenation [45,51–53] to 
371 
improve the stability of RFCC gasoline. However, the process leads to an undesirable 
372 
decrease in the gasoline octane, resulting from converting high-octane olefins into low-octane 
373 
paraffin [51,52,54,55]. Recently, Dubkov et al. [46] used nitrous oxide (N2O), a greenhouse 
374 
gas, to oxidise light-catalytic cracked gasoline. The treatment of the gasoline with N2O was 
375 
performed without catalyst in the liquid phase at 200 – 250oC and elevated pressure of 50-70 
376 
atm. The findings suggest that N2O selectively oxidises olefinic compounds in the gasoline to 
377 
form carbonyl products and molecular nitrogen without affecting the gasoline components, 
378 
making N2O a good candidate for olefin reduction in gasoline. 
379 
In 1996, the Kashima refinery in Japan revamped its FCC unit by modifying it to a 
380 
new UOP RFCC. The modifications include (a) a catalyst cooler, (b) an expanded air blower 
381 
and gas compressor, (c) improved atomisation feed nozzles, and (d) a design with a short 
382 
contact time (SCT) riser with a cyclone riser directly connected to riser termination. The 
383 
revamp allowed the FCC unit to increase throughput from 20,000 to 28,500 bpsd with a 
384 
higher intake of residue feedstocks. Modified unit and adjusted feed rate and composition 
385 
produced gasoline with high olefin content. A switch to a new catalyst by Akzo Nobel was 
386 
designed to reduce olefin content while maintaining the RON of the gasoline. The new 
387 
catalyst design for controlling gasoline olefins reduced olefins concentration in gasoline by 
388 
more than 5 v% without a significant RON decrease [56]. 
389 
3.2 
Residue fluid conversion catalyst 
390 
Due to the increasing scarcity of crude oil resources, the feed to the residue fluid 
391 
catalytic cracking unit has become heavier and laden with heavy metals, for example, 
392 

<<<PAGE 22>>>
 22
vanadium and nickel, which are harmful to the life of the catalyst. For this reason, superior 
393 
technology has been developed to improve heavy metal pollution and render the catalyst 
394 
matrix less active to the effects of nickel and/or vanadium [30,37,57].  
395 
Zeolite-Y, with a uniform microporous structure and an inlet diameter of 0.74 nm, is 
396 
the main active component of the catalyst used in the conventional FCC process [38,58,59]. 
397 
However, the large molecular diameter (1.2 to 15 nm) of atmospheric residue, which serves 
398 
as feed to the modern RFCC process [37], makes it nearly impossible for the large molecules 
399 
in heavy oil to access the micropores of the zeolite to be cracked directly [38,60,61]. 
400 
Therefore, it is desired that RFCC catalyst for processing heavy residue feedstocks must have 
401 
improved diffusion abilities, enhanced acid site accessibilities and tolerance to metal poisons 
402 
[62–67]. Much research has been undertaken to introduce meso- and macroporosities into 
403 
zeolite crystals through developing hierarchical zeolite materials and synthesising metal-
404 
resistant catalysts [68,69]. 
405 
Recently, attention has been paid to core-shell structured catalysts because they can 
406 
provide concrete reaction pathways and improve the synergy of many active sites on the 
407 
formulated catalyst [70,71]. For this reason, many core-shell structured composites that 
408 
employ zeolite crystal as core and mesoporous matrix as shell are developed to process 
409 
residue feedstocks more efficiently [62,63,65–67]. This core-shell composite may improve 
410 
mass transfer limitations, but still, there are challenges concerning metals poisoning. 
411 
3.3 
Catalytic cracking catalyst for improving propylene production 
412 
Propylene is an important petrochemical feedstock for producing products such as 
413 
acrylonitrile, butyl alcohol, cumene, phenol, polypropylene, and propylene oxide [72]. 
414 
Propylene production is broadly categorised into steam and catalytic cracking paths [73]. The 
415 
steam cracking path is the highest energy-consuming process in the petrochemical industry. 
416 

<<<PAGE 23>>>
 23
The type of feedstock and operating conditions considerably impact propylene production in 
417 
the steam cracking process. The reaction temperature can reach 800oC for steam cracking by 
418 
releasing large amounts of CO2. The catalytic cracking path operates at a lower temperature 
419 
of around 500oC and has higher propylene production [74]. 
420 
RFCC technology for higher propylene production has drawn much attention because 
421 
of its low-cost and abundant feedstock compared with conventional steam cracking 
422 
technology [75]. A proper catalyst design and preparation are required for optimal propylene 
423 
production. Higher propylene production can be achieved by adjusting the acid quantity, 
424 
strength and type of RFCC catalysts [73,76]. 
425 
To improve propylene production, Liu et al. [77] investigated an RFCC catalyst for 
426 
maximising propylene production by mixing the modified NaY/kaolin composite 
427 
microspheres and additive microspheres containing ZSM-5. The characterisation results 
428 
showed that the catalyst contained higher meso- and macro-pores, had higher acid sites than 
429 
the reference catalyst, and increased propylene production by approximately 1%. In a similar 
430 
attempt, Gao et al. [78] investigated phosphorus-modified ZSM-5 prepared via hydrothermal 
431 
dispersion in the cracking of light diesel for maximising propylene production. The result 
432 
showed that more pronounced cracking activity was obtained on the catalyst containing 
433 
phosphorus, and propylene production increased by 3% compared to the reference ZSM-5 
434 
catalyst. The increase in propylene production can be attributed to the higher amount of 
435 
phosphorus fixed on the zeolite prepared by hydrothermal dispersion and the higher residual 
436 
ratio of crystallinity. 
437 
Numerous studies have also investigated the suitability of hierarchical ZSM-5 for 
438 
improving propylene production [79–82]. Hierarchical ZSM-5 has shown excellent shape-
439 
selective catalytic activity owing to its porous structure. It also offers good hydrothermal 
440 
stability and strong resistance to coking. The stability of the catalyst is attributed to the 
441 

<<<PAGE 24>>>
 24
aluminium content and the shape selectivity given by the microporous network of the zeolite 
442 
cavities. In contrast, the coke tolerance is attributed to the hierarchical nature of the catalyst, 
443 
which makes it difficult for the catalyst pores to be blocked [79–82].  
444 
Bulatov and Jirnov [83] investigated the conversion of heavy feed at various 
445 
concentrations of ZSM-5 additive between 0 and 40 wt % at a catalyst-to-oil ratio of 28, a 
446 
riser outlet pressure, temperature and contact time of 0.0739 Mpa, 566oC, and 1.5 seconds 
447 
respectively. An increase in the amount of ZSM-5 to significantly high concentrations, up to 
448 
40 wt%, was found to have little effect on propylene production. The propylene yield 
449 
increased by around 10% of ZSM-5 crystal concentration in the catalyst inventory. It was 
450 
concluded that the decrease in the effectiveness of ZSM-5 at higher concentrations transpired 
451 
mainly due to the exhaustion of the gasoline olefin precursor. 
452 
Konno et al. [84] studied the effect of zeolite crystal size on ZSM-5 (Si/Al = 150) 
453 
catalytic stability in n-hexane under atmospheric pressure at temperatures ranging between 
454 
550oC and 650oC. The findings showed that the Si/Al ratio of the zeolite affected the product 
455 
selectivity and production of propylene. In the study, ZSM-5 with different Si/Al ratios of 50, 
456 
150 and 300 were prepared via hydrothermal synthesis in a water/surfactant/organic solvent. 
457 
It was found that ZSM-5 with Si/Al of 150 exhibited the highest propylene production s of 
458 
50.4% at 650oC because excessive reactions such as the consumption of light olefins and 
459 
BTX formation were suppressed by the low acid density (high Si/Al ratio). It was concluded 
460 
that applying ZSM-5 zeolite (Si/Al = 150) to the cracking reaction at a high reaction 
461 
temperature of 650oC improved propylene yield.

3.4 
Functional additives for catalytic cracking 
463 
Functional additives are catalyst materials added separately to the base RFCC 
464 
catalyst, blended with the catalyst during formulation or even introduced into the feedstock. 
465 

<<<PAGE 25>>>
 25
The main benefits of these additives are to increase gasoline octane number, increase 
466 
propylene production, minimise metals contamination of the catalyst and reduce the number 
467 
of pollutants emitted from the RFCC regenerator [85–89]. 
468 
The combustion of coke in the RFCC regenerator produces several atmospheric 
469 
pollutants that need to be controlled. These pollutants include carbon monoxide (CO), sulfur 
470 
oxides (SOx), nitrogen oxides (NOx), and particulate matter (PM). However, additives are 
471 
customised with particular properties based on requirements without altering product 
472 
distribution. 
473 
A practical approach toward NOx emission mitigation from fluid catalytic cracking 
474 
units was investigated by reviewing options for cost-efficient and pragmatic emissions 
475 
mitigation using optimal amounts of precious metals while evaluating the potential benefits 
476 
of typical promoter dopant packages [90]. The findings suggest that at the present catalyst 
477 
development level, the refinery is no longer forced to make a promoter selection based on 
478 
preconceived notions regarding precious metal activity but can rather make decisions based 
479 
on the best total cost financial impact to the operation without measurable loss of the 
480 
CO/NOx emission selectivity. 
481 
Mo et al. [91] studied SOx reduction additives for partial burn FCC by employing a 
482 
commercially available SOx reduction additive and an additive developed by JM Savannah. 
483 
The authors found that among other SOx reduction technologies, the use of additives in FCC 
484 
units provides a simple and cost-effective method and has been found essential by most 
485 
refiners. It was also pointed out that the reduction of SOx by additives was achieved via SOx 
486 
capture in the regenerator and the release of H2S after being transferred to the riser.  
487 
Chester et al. [92] carried out a study on CO combustion promoter additives in FCC 
488 
regenerator unit by employing CO promoter containing various noble metals (platinum (Pt), 
489 
Palladium (Pd), Iridium (Ir), Rhodium (Rh), Osborne (Os) and Rubidium (Ru)). The findings 
490 

<<<PAGE 26>>>
 26
suggest that when 10 ppm of Pt, Pd and Rh were used, oxidation activity (CO2/CO) were 49, 
491 
36 and 26, respectively and when 5 ppm of Ir, Ru and Os were used, CO2/CO were 43, 8.6 
492 
and 2.2 respectively. It was concluded that CO combustion promoters containing Pt are the 
493 
best at oxidising CO to CO2, meaning that CO promoters containing Pt should be employed 
494 
when controlling the burning problem in the FCC regenerator after considering the cost.

4 
RFCC Catalyst Deactivation  
554 
Refineries frequently find an opportunity to buy low-value or lower-cost crude 
555 
feedstocks to reduce operating costs. Although these opportunity feeds can help improve 
556 
profit margins, they usually have serious consequences. These consequences include the 
557 
destruction of catalysts and the production of a large amount of hydrogen and coke at the 
558 
expense of gasoline due to unspecified concentrations of impurities in the feed [14,100–102]. 
559 
Therefore, it is vital to be aware of such impurities to avoid surprises, such as catalyst 
560 

<<<PAGE 32>>>
 32
deactivation and high coke deposition, which can cause indiscriminate product distribution 
561 
and shorten the cycle length of cracking catalysts. 
562 
Modern RFCC cracking catalyst deactivates rapidly at temperatures above 730-745°C 
563 
due to hydrothermal deactivation caused by high temperatures and a steam environment. The 
564 
high temperature is responsible for the catalyst's sintering (melts and collapses) [103]. 
565 
Cracking catalyst also deactivates by the sodium and vanadium that accumulates on 
566 
the catalyst. Sodium neutralises some of the acid sites within the zeolite structure, rendering 
567 
those acid sites permanently inactive. Sodium poisoning is usually caused by sodium chloride 
568 
that enters the crude unit during a desalter upset. However, it is well controlled in most 
569 
refineries using double desalters in the crude unit. The fresh catalyst typically has 2000 to 
570 
3000 ppm sodium left from manufacturing. Additive sodium beyond 5000 ppm on an 
571 
equilibrium catalyst can cause a rapid decline in catalyst activity [104]. 
572 
Vanadium, when deposited on the catalyst particle surface, oxidises in the regenerator 
573 
to V2O5, which in the presence of steam becomes mobile vanadic acid (H3VO4) that migrates 
574 
throughout the catalyst particle and then destroys the zeolite silica-alumina framework 
575 
[105,106]. Vanadium is organically bound within organometallic molecules and tends to 
576 
concentrate in the heaviest fraction of the feedstock. Modern vanadium trapping technology 
577 
allows concentrations of up to 7000 ppm vanadium on equilibrium catalyst to be tolerated 
578 
while maintaining suitable catalyst activity [107–109].  
579 
Nickel is also organically bound within organometallic molecules and tends to 
580 
concentrate in the heaviest fraction of the feedstock. Nickel is a dehydrogenation agent and 
581 
catalyses the formation of H2 and coke. However, nickel deposits on the surface of the 
582 
catalyst particle and, once deposited, tend to be stationary. The signature symptom of nickel 
583 
poisoning is high H2 formation, which results in high dry gas production. The formation of a 
584 
high amount of hydrogen can be countered by modern nickel trapping technology, lift gas

Over the years, the poisoning effect of basic nitrogen has been extensively published 
594 
[100,111,112,123–125]. Recently, there has been a shift in crude oil supply towards heavier 
595 
crudes. Heavy crude contains a high concentration of nitrogen (8000 ppm and above) 
596 
compared to lighter crude (0 – 5000 ppm). This shift necessitates the need to devise a means 
597 
of dealing with this sharp increase in nitrogen content in the processing of the heavy crude 
598 
feedstock. High-nitrogen crudes have nitrogen content greater than 0.25 wt%, and nitrogen-
599 
poor crudes with nitrogen content lower than 0.25 wt% [126]. In the lighter fractions of crude 
600 
oil, nitrogen is mainly in the form of basic compounds (pyridines, quinolines and 
601 
benzoquinolines). On the contrary, the nitrogen sources in heavy crude oil are in the form of 
602 
non-basic compounds such as indoles, pyrroles and carbazoles [110]. 
603 
Two mechanisms have been used to explain the retarding effects of basic nitrogen 
604 
compounds [111,127]. First, the basic nitrogen compounds reversibly absorb on the Brønsted 
605 
or Lewis acid centres and then poison the acid centres hindering the hydrocarbon cracking 
606 
ability of the FCC catalyst. Second, because of their big size and aromatic nature, the basic 
607 
nitrogen compounds may be the precursors of coke. Li et al. [113] found that the retarding 
608 
effects of non-basic compounds and condensed aromatics in coker gas oil catalytic cracking 
609 
are less serious than those of basic nitrogen compounds. Fig. 5 depicts the proposed 
610 
mechanism for the inductive partial poisoning effect of nitrogen on neighbouring acid sites

4.2 
Sulfur poisoning 
622 
Sulfur is regarded as the amplest heteroatom in crude oil. In most cases, the 
623 
concentration of sulfur in crude increases with a decrease in API gravity, which means that 
624 
the heavier the crude, the greater the concentration of sulfur [115,116,130]. RFCC catalyst 
625 
sulfur comes mainly from the cracking of long alkyl chain thiophenes. The mechanisms of 
626 
thiophene hydrogenation and H2S production are presented in Fig. 6. 
627 
Alkylated thiophenes, benzothiophenes and dibenzothiophenes are the major sulfur 
628 
compounds in typical RFCC feeds [12,45]. The deactivation mechanism of RFCC catalyst by 
629 
sulfur molecules occurs through interaction with acid sites and condensation reactions of 
630 
their unsaturated rings leading to coke formation [12,131]. The coke formed can poison the 
631 
acid sites or block the pore structure. 
632 
Sulfur is present in hydrocarbon feedstock in the form of hydrogen sulfide, sulfur 
633 
dioxide, thiophene, and its derivatives. These sulfur compounds contribute significantly to 
634 
catalyst poisoning. However, it is thought that the poisoning mechanisms of sulfur 
635 
compounds are different and lead to a reduction in catalyst performance through different 
636 
deactivation pathways. Fig. 7 illustrates the three major sulfur deactivation mechanisms 
637 
commonly encountered in hydrocarbon processing. The mechanisms include the sulfidation, 
638 
alteration and coke formation mechanisms[129]. 
639 

<<<PAGE 39>>>
 39
The sulfidation step (Fig. 7a) involves the direct interactions between sulfur and 
640 
catalyst active sites. These include the adsorption of sulfur compounds and the formation of 
641 
sulfides. Catalysts exposed to low H2S concentrations are typically deactivated via the 
642 
adsorption of sulfur onto the catalyst surfaces, thereby blocking off access of the reactants to 
643 
the active metal sites. The adsorption is usually reversible, and catalytic activity can be 
644 
regenerated by raising the temperature or lowering the feed sulfur content to desorb the sulfur 
645 
from the poisoned surfaces. At higher H2S concentrations, sulfur's chemical potential 
646 
increases, favouring the chemisorption of sulfur into the catalyst material, leading to 
647 
subsequent sulfidation reactions to give metal sulfides. The sulfidation reactions of transition 
648 
metal active sites are generally irreversible and can result in permanent loss in activity of the 
649 
catalyst, as demonstrated in Fig. 7b. 
650 
In the alteration step (Fig. 7b), sulfur compounds interact with the active sites of the 
651 
catalyst and deactivate the preferred catalyst sites, thereby leading to a change in catalyst 
652 
functions and activities. This poisoning can alter the functionality of catalysts by affecting the 
653 
affinity, selectivity and reaction pathways of adsorbed reactants on the surface of the metal 
654 
sites. The coke formation step (Fig. 7c) involves the role of sulfur compounds in the growth 
655 
and deposition of carbonaceous coke on the catalyst, thereby reducing the activity of the 
656 
catalyst. Various studies have suggested that coke formation is accelerated for sulphur-
657 
poisoned catalysts due to preferential interactions of alkyl radicals with the sulphur atoms to 
658 
form R-S species. The R-S species then rapidly dehydrogenate to give indirectly-bonded coke 
659 
deposits on the surface of the catalysts’ active sites, which are very stable [132,133].

4.3 
Iron poisoning 
661 
Iron deposits on the RFCC catalyst as organic iron from the feed or inorganic iron 
662 
from equipment corrosion. When deposited on the RFCC catalyst, this Fe promotes 
663 

<<<PAGE 40>>>
 40
undesirable dehydrogenation reaction and catalyst circulation problems due to the formation 
664 
of nodules on the catalyst surface. Rainer et al. [134] suggested that when the RFCC catalyst 
665 
was contaminated with Fe, analysis using Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) indicated a 
666 
bumpy and nodulated appearance on the surface of the RFCC catalyst, leading to catalyst 
667 
circulation problems. Similarly, Psarras et al. [135] noted that as the level of iron increases on 
668 
the surface of the RFCC catalyst, the sintering phenomenon results in the imposition of 
669 
diffusional barriers to the migration of heavy molecules of a hydrocarbon feedstock to the 
670 
active site of the catalyst and this led to poor performance of the RFCC unit. The relationship 
671 
between RFCC unit performance and iron contamination is well-established in the literature 
672 
[136,137]. For example, it has been well-established that iron deposits on FCC catalysts form 
673 
a dense layer on the catalysts’ surface, and the dense layer stops reactants from diffusing to 
674 
the inner structures of the catalyst. This causes adverse effects on the catalyst's ability to 
675 
convert heavy oil and could greatly reduce gasoline yield while increasing yields of dry gas 
676 
and coke. The reaction performance of the catalyst would be severely affected when the iron 
677 
content on the equilibrium catalyst exceeds 8000 ppm [29,138]. 
678 
4.4 
Sodium poisoning 
679 
Sodium is an important heteroatom that is part of the hydrocarbon structure. It may 
680 
also be from salty water. It is a deleterious cause of poisoning to watch out for in the RFCC 
681 
feed. Sodium comes from various sources, including naturally contaminated oil feedstocks, a 
682 
remnant from oil extraction due to a faulty desalter, or a residual material from catalyst 
683 
preparation. Sodium is well known to cause the destruction of zeolite framework, participate 
684 
in the ionic exchange and reduce the amount of Bronsted acid sites [104,105,139]. Oliveira et 
685 
al. [140] and Xu et al. [141] reported that Na and V could interact synergistically with steam 
686 

<<<PAGE 41>>>
 41
to yield NaOH. The NaOH formed migrates into the zeolite structure and readily dissolves 
687 
crystalline components, negatively affecting the catalyst‘s activity and selectivity.

709

713

714

diffusion of feed, while optimised catalyst surface pores have excellent feed diffusion. In 
743 
minimal catalyst surface pores, the threshold for added iron is usually low in the range of 0.3 
744 
wt% compared to optimised catalyst surface pores, with a threshold of added iron of 1.5 wt% 
745 
and above. These optimised catalyst pores make the catalyst withstand the harmful effect of 
746 
iron by providing additional pores to accommodate the pore-blocking effect of iron. Another 
747 
way to mitigate the detrimental impact of iron is to increase the fresh catalyst additions into 
748 
the regenerator to flush iron out of the circulating inventory. This method is usually very 
749 
expensive to practice due to the high cost of catalysts, as it consumes a lot of cracking 
750 
catalysts [150,151]. Typically, catalyst consumption is 1200 tons/day, but using this method, 
751 
the consumption could be as high as 2000 tons/day. Therefore, this approach must be 
752 

<<<PAGE 47>>>
 47
optimised with iron poisoning as a potential objective function and catalyst cost as a 
753 
constraint [152]. 
754 
5.2 
Sodium mitigation 
755 
Sodium acts as a permanent catalyst poison; therefore, a fresh catalyst with low 
756 
sodium should be employed to mitigate the effect of sodium. Another reliable way to reduce 
757 
the sodium effect is to improve and optimise desalter operations to remove sodium from the 
758 
feed. Refineries such as the Dangote petroleum refinery and petrochemicals in Nigeria 
759 
employ a double desalter in their crude distillation unit to tackle the sodium effect. Sodium 
760 
can also be mitigated by increasing the catalyst addition to flush sodium out of the circulating 
761 
inventory. This increases fresh catalyst activity to combat activity loss from sodium. 
762 
However, this method is not sustainable as it is very expensive to practice due to the high 
763 
cost of catalysts [153].  
764 
5.3 
Nickel mitigation  
765 
Ni becomes a concern at an equilibrium catalyst concentration greater than 800 ppm 
766 
[154]. Some refiners have used antimony (Sb) injection to passivate the harmful effect of 
767 
nickel. However, antimony has been found to increase NOx emissions, bottom fouling and 
768 
cause some environmental concerns regarding the safe handling of antimony [154]. 
769 
A more reliable way to control nickel’s effect is using speciality alumina, bismuth and 
770 
rare earth compounds. Alumina and bismuth passivate nickel by forming nickel alloy on the 
771 
catalyst surface, which is difficult to reduce to more detrimental elemental nickel. While 
772 
these have proved successful to some extent, speciality alumina and bismuth are immobile 
773 
under RFCC conditions. They cannot migrate to the nickel that deposits peripherally on the 
774 
catalyst’s surface, reducing their effectiveness. Rare earth compounds, on the other hand, are 
775 

<<<PAGE 48>>>
 48
very expensive for application in the refinery despite their success at mitigating the harmful 
776 
effect of nickel. 
777 
The shortcomings of antimony, speciality alumina, bismuth and rare earth compounds 
778 
are overcome using boron-based technology, identified as the most reliable technology to 
779 
mitigate the effect of nickel on RFCC catalysts. Boron is mobile under RFCC conditions, 
780 
migrating to the nickel and occupying the interstitial spaces forming Ni-B alloy. This Ni-B 
781 
alloy is difficult to reduce to a detrimental form under riser conditions, thereby mitigating the 
782 
dehydrogenating effect of nickel. Nickel does not increase NOx emissions and is safe to 
783 
handle and environmentally friendly [155]. 
784 
5.4 
Vanadium mitigation 
785 
Vanadium typically becomes a concern at an equilibrium catalyst concentration 
786 
greater than 1500 ppm [155]. Refiners can use vanadium additives to mitigate the harmful 
787 
effect of vanadium. These additives include rare earth oxides (REO), calcium and 
788 
magnesium-based. Tin has also been identified as having the potential to mitigate vanadium 
789 
poisons. It is important to note that these additives are added separately or blended into the 
790 
catalyst formulation during the synthesis. 
791 
Low sodium fresh catalyst should be employed to mitigate the effect of vanadium 
792 
because the harmful impact of vanadium is exaggerated in the presence of sodium. Another 
793 
way to mitigate the effect of vanadium is to increase fresh catalyst additions into the 
794 
regenerator or use purchased catalysts to flush out the vanadium in the circulating 
795 
inventory. While this method is successful, it is not advisable to practice because it is very 
796 
expensive [155].

5.5 
Passivators for the simultaneous mitigation of nickel and vanadium poisons 
798 
The various passivators and mitigation strategies of the individual metal contaminants 
799 
(sodium, iron, nickel and vanadium) that affect the RFCC catalyst have been discussed in 5.1 
800 
to 5.4. Some metal passivators can also be combined into a single catalyst to utilise their 
801 
synergistic effects for mitigating multiple metal poisons. Despite the importance of using a 
802 
single passivator to address the deleterious effect of nickel and vanadium, far too little 
803 
attention has been paid to this area of research. Hoffer et al. [6] studied heavy metal 
804 
passivators/traps for the FCC process by combining antimony (Sb) and iron (Fe). The authors 
805 
reported that when Sb/Fe passivator was incorporated into an FCC catalyst contaminated 
806 
with nickel, H2 production was reduced by 15%. When the same combination was 
807 
incorporated in the FCC catalyst deactivated with vanadium, H2 production declined by 20%. 
808 
Finally, when these metals combination was used to passivate FCC catalyst co-deactivated 
809 
with nickel and vanadium, H2 and coke production was reduced by 30%. The authors 
810 
concluded that the combination of Sb and Fe successfully mitigated the deleterious effects of 
811 
nickel and vanadium. However, Fe is a dehydrogenating poison and is unsuitable for 
812 
mitigating the dehydrogenation effect of nickel and vanadium. 
813 
 In a similar vein, Hayes and Castillo [156] used Sb/Sn to passivate nickel and 
814 
vanadium-contaminated cracking catalysts. The findings suggested that H2 yield decreased by 
815 
17% compared to 10% when Sb alone was used. It was concluded that a combination of Sb 
816 
and Sn produces even better passivation than Sb when used alone. However, Sb as a 
817 
passivator was found to increase NOx emission and causes fouling of downstream 
818 
equipment.

826 
It should be noted that “passivator” and “trap” are not used interchangeably and that 
827 
the mixture of metal oxides comprises components that either passivate or trap the metal 
828 
impurities. “Passivator” is a structure that decreases the activity of undesirable metals such as 
829 
nickel and vanadium. A “trap” is a composition that immobilises contaminant metals that  
830

831

832

833

834

835 
RFCC process.

6.1.1 Antimony (Sb) as nickel passivator 
846 
The use of antimony compounds as nickel passivator has been studied by several 
847 
authors, as detailed in Table 7 [7,154,157]. In general, the source poison is nickel in the form 
848 
of naphthenates, while antimony oxide is employed as the passivating compound. During the 
849 
impregnation process of antimony compounds on the FCC catalyst, the drying temperature 
850 
has been established to range between 100 – 204oC for 1 hour, calcination temperature in the 
851 
range of 590 – 677oC for 2 – 14 hours and all catalysts are steam treated in 100% steam 
852 
environment at a temperature ranging between 732 – 766oC for 8 – 18 hours. 
853 
Corma et al. [154] studied nickel passivation on fluidised cracking catalysts with 
854 
different antimony complexes by impregnating the FCC catalyst with nickel naphthenates 
855 
and impregnating the same with antimony oxide as passivator. The authors reported that 
856 
when FCC catalyst was impregnated with nickel naphthenates, gasoline yield decreased by 5 
857 
wt% while H2 and coke increased by 0.5 wt%. Upon passivation of the nickel-deactivated 
858 
catalyst with antimony oxide, the formation of Ni-Sb alloy led to a decrease in coke and dry 
859 
gas production of 16% and 8%, respectively. 
860 
Goldwasser et al. [7] studied Ni-Sb interaction in a rare earth Y zeolite by 
861 
impregnating FCC catalyst with nickel naphthenate and passivating the nickel-deactivated 
862 
catalyst with antimony oxide. It was reported that there was an increase in H2 and coke yield 
863 
with increasing nickel loading of 1%. A strong decrease in percentage conversion was also 
864 
observed with the addition of nickel to the catalyst. The addition of an antimony compound 
865 
reinstated the transformation and production of H2 and coke otherwise obtained with an 
866 
active unpoisoned catalyst. 
867 

<<<PAGE 55>>>
 55
Antimony, when used as a nickel-passivator, demonstrated a substantial decrease in 
868 
the nickel poisoning of an RFCC/FCC catalyst [16]. The interaction of antimony with nickel 
869 
has been studied extensively in the past four decades. FCC catalysts with a nickel loading 
870 
range of 1.9 and 4.4% weight and Sb to Ni ratios between 0.00 and 0.41 were examined 
871 
[168]. The X-ray diffraction findings suggested that Ni-Sb solid matrices are formed with an 
872 
increased level of Sb appearing on the nickel surface. In explaining the results, however, 
873 
geometric and electronic effects were reported as follows:  
874 
(i) 
Alteration of nickel surface atoms: the electronic properties of nickel surface 
875

876 
(ii) 
Quantity of antimony available for passivation: the equilibrium between the 
877 
interaction of antimony, nickel and support determines the amount of antimony 
878 
available to passivate nickel.  
879 
(iii) 
Blockage of nickel sites: antimony geometrically blocks nickel sites in the 
880 
catalyst. 
881 
The interaction between nickel and antimony was studied in a Rare-Earth (Lanthanum 
882 
oxide)-exchanged zeolite-Y [7]. Furthermore, the influence of the addition of antimony on 
883

884

885

886

887

888

889

890

891

6.1.2 Rare earth compounds as nickel passivator  
894 
Rare-earth compounds have been employed to passivate the deleterious effect of 
895 
nickel [120,134]. Nickel naphthenates are the major sources of nickel poison, and rare-earth 
896 
oxides such as cerium oxide and lanthanum oxide are the passivators used to mitigate the 
897 
harmful effect of nickel. The operating conditions are similar to those used in antimony 
898 
mitigation of nickel poisons. Drying temperature ranges from 80 – 100oC for 1 hour, 
899 
calcination temperature ranges from 600 – 649oC for 1 – 2 hours, while steam treatment is 
900 
carried out in a 100% steam environment at a temperature between 788 – 800oC for 6.5 – 10 
901 
hours. 
902 
Escobar et al. [120] investigated the interaction between Ni and V with ultra-stable-Y 
903 
and rare-earth-Y zeolite during hydrothermal deactivation by impregnating FCC catalyst with 
904 
nickel naphthenate and passivating same with rare-earth oxides. It was observed that nickel 
905 
alone does not affect the loss in zeolite surface area except in the presence of vanadium. 
906 
There was 59% preservation in zeolite surface area with the introduction of rare earth oxides 
907 
and 44% surface area preservation by rare earth oxides when the catalyst was poisoned by 
908 
both vanadium and nickel. It was concluded that the presence of nickel reduces the 
909 
effectiveness of rare earth oxides. 
910 
An investigation into the passivation of FCC catalysts showed that when 3000 ppm of 
911 
cerium oxide was used to passivate FCC catalysts contaminated with 3000 ppm of vanadium 
912 
and 1500 ppm of nickel, hydrogen production increased by 2% instead of an expected 
913 
decrease. In contrast, when 3000 ppm of cerium octoate was used to passivate the FCC 
914 
catalyst, the production decreased by 6%. It was concluded that cerium in organic form 
915 
(octoate) significantly reduced the amount of hydrogen produced, which is attributed to 
916 

<<<PAGE 57>>>
 57
nickel contamination [158]. However, cerium is highly expensive and hence not cost-
917 
effective. This limits its potential use.

6.1.3 Bismuth as nickel passivator 
919 
Due to the environmental challenges associated with antimony and the high cost of 
920 
rare earth compounds as nickel passivators, bismuth was used as a replacement for both 
921 
antimony and rare earth compounds to passivate the harmful effect of nickel. During the 
922 
deactivation and passivation process, the drying and calcination temperatures are kept at 
923 
120oC for 1 hour and 550oC for 5 hours, respectively. The regenerator temperature was 
924 
682oC for 3 – 30 minutes, a catalyst-to-oil ratio of 4:1 and riser temperature, pressure and 
925 
contact time were kept at 593oC, 3.50 kg/cm2 and 15 seconds, respectively. 
926 
Bismuth compounds developed by Gulf petroleum have been reported as effective 
927 
nickel passivating agents [103]. To the best of our knowledge, there is no published 
928 
information on the chemistry of the interaction of bismuth with nickel. However, various 
929 
reports have been published on the commercial benefits of bismuth additives compared to 
930 
antimony [103]. Bismuth has been chosen as a preferred nickel passivating agent because it is 
931 
less toxic than antimony, as the United States Environmental Protection Agency does not list 
932 
it as a hazardous chemical.  
933 
Ramamoorthy and Krishna. [159] in their patent studied the method of suppressing 
934 
metal contaminants' harmful effects on cracking catalysts in FCC by contacting a nickel-
935 
contaminated hydrocarbon feedstock with a cracking catalyst associated with a bismuth-
936 
containing metal passivating agent. The findings suggest that the suppression of nickel 
937 
poisoning can be accomplished with a proportionally smaller amount of bismuth. In contrast, 
938 
higher amounts of bismuth are less effective and provide adverse effects such as poor 
939 
deposition efficiencies on the catalyst. Meanwhile, high bismuth levels can end up in cracked 
940 
cycle oil products. These, in turn, can cause adverse effects on the downstream 
941 

<<<PAGE 58>>>
 58
hydroprocessing catalysts when the cycle oils are processed further. It was deduced that the 
942 
best weight ratio of bismuth to nickel equivalents should be in the range of 0.01:1 to 1:1 in 
943 
the feed to be effective [159].

6.1.4 Boron-based technology as nickel passivator 
945 
The deposition profile of nickel follows a particular gradient: nickel deposits 
946 
peripherally on the catalyst's surface. Therefore, it requires a mobile passivator under RFCC 
947 
conditions to migrate to the catalyst's surface and effectively passivate nickel. In addition to 
948 
being environmentally friendly, boron is mobile under RFCC conditions, migrating to the 
949 
nickel and occupying the interstitial positions in Ni-B alloy [169]. This alloy is difficult to 
950 
reduce to a more detrimental nickel in the RFCC riser. Therefore, it passivates the harmful 
951 
effect of nickel. Similar to the previous process conditions of other nickel passivators, the 
952 
drying temperature is kept at 80 – 100oC for 1 – 2 hours. Calcination temperature and time of 
953 
540 – 550oC and 5 hours, respectively, while the steam treatment is done at 760oC for 6 hours 
954 
in a 100% steam environment. 
955 
Boron-based catalysts can overcome the (i) NOx emissions of antimony, (ii) high cost 
956 
of cerium and (iii) immobility of bismuth. Boron is mobile under RFCC conditions, which 
957 
makes it more efficient as it migrates within the catalyst to passivate nickel. BASF, a well-
958 
known catalyst manufacturing company, has studied boron chemistry to evaluate the Ni 
959 
passivation mechanism. Based on multiple spectroscopic studies using boron-based 
960 
technology, Ni can be kept in a less reducible state, which inhibits dehydrogenation reactions. 
961 
Thus, it lowers the formation of hydrogen and contaminant coke. A boron-based technology 
962 
as an innovative solution for RFCC performance improvement was studied [155].  
963 
A catalyst (BoroCat) was designed, and upon application of the catalyst to the RFCC 
964 
unit, there was a 26% reduction in unwanted hydrogen, 16% coke reduction and a 3% 
965 
increase in LPG and gasoline yields at higher conversion. A new spectroscopic methodology 
966 

<<<PAGE 59>>>
 59
was used in characterising the impact of boron interaction on RFCC catalysts contaminated 
967 
with nickel via trend analysis of carbon monoxide diffuse reflective infrared Fourier 
968 
transform spectroscopy (CO DRIFTS) [161]. The findings suggest that the characterisation of 
969 
boron is difficult due to its low atomic weight. CO DRIFTS with three pre-treatment 
970 
temperatures can be used to characterise the impact of boron on the catalyst. It was concluded 
971 
that boron-based technology passivates nickel by decreasing its reducibility under typical 
972 
RFCC conditions at a temperature of less than 600oC.

6.2.1 Tin compounds as vanadium passivator

Tin compounds have been identified as the most common passivators of the harmful 
976 
effects of vanadium [162-164]. The source of vanadium poison and passivator has been 
977 
established to be vanadyl naphthenate and tetraphenyl tin, respectively. The operating 
978 
conditions for vanadium deactivation and passivation of FCC catalyst are within the same 
979 
range as those of nickel, except that most of the steam treatments were carried out at 95% 
980 
steam, with the remaining percentage being either N2 or Argon. 
981 
Anderson et al. [164] in their study of tin passivation of vanadium in a metal-
982 
contaminated fluid cracking catalyst, carried out steaming in a 95% steam environment and 
983 
5% N2 instead of 100% steam. The 5% inert medium makes the reactor system partially inert 
984 
to prevent side reactions that will interfere with the steaming process. It was found that 
985 
vanadium present as free VO2+ cations favour the breakdown of zeolite lattice. The order of 
986 
deposition of tin and vanadium was found to exhibit a little effect on the nature of the 
987 
resulting species that passivates the deleterious effect of vanadium. 
988 
Bradley et al. [162] carried out a spectroscopic study of tin passivation of vanadium 
989 
contaminants in a model fluid cracking catalyst. It was observed that an irreversible loss of 
990 

<<<PAGE 60>>>
 60
crystallinity led to the collapse of the zeolite framework. Retention of crystallinity was 
991 
detected after adding tetraphenyl tin after steam-ageing, and that tin was found to limit 
992 
vanadium-zeolite interactions.  
993 
The passivation of vanadium using tin in metal-contaminated fluid cracking catalysts 
994 
was conducted using tin Mossbauer spectroscopy. The work investigated the role of tin in the 
995 
passivation of vanadium contaminants in model systems comprising aluminosilicate gel, EuY 
996 
and EuY-gel mixtures [163]. Tin was added to the catalyst as tetraphenyl tin (Sn(Ph)4) and 
997 
remained predominantly intact on the zeolite. However, it formed oxygen ligands on the gel 
998 
replacing one or two phenyl groups. On the zeolite, a portion of the tin is left over on the 
999 
surface while some portion migrates to internal sites. In the zeolite-gel mixture, tin is 
1000 
favourably adsorbed by the zeolite. It was observed that Sn (II), metallic tin, and vanadium-
1001 
tin alloys were not produced at all stages of catalyst pre-treatment. Nonetheless, calcination 
1002 
and steaming formed vanadium/tin oxide complexes built over oxygen bridges in which tin is 
1003 
in the +4-oxidation state. The formation of Sn4+-O-V5+ linkages is possibly liable for the 
1004 
passivation of V by tetraphenyl tin deposition. 
1005 
6.2.2 Rare-earth compounds as vanadium passivator  
1006 
In addition to being able to passivate nickel, rare earth compounds such as lanthanum 
1007 
octoate, dysprosium and samarium naphthenates have proven successful at passivating the 
1008 
deleterious effect of vanadium [166,167]. The high cost of these metals may limit their 
1009 
potential use. The operating conditions for deactivation and passivation of FCC catalyst by 
1010 
vanadium and rare earth compounds are similar, as depicted in Table 7. The drying 
1011 
temperature is maintained at 80 – 100oC for 24 hours, calcination temperature of 500 – 600oC 
1012 
for 2 – 5 hours and finally, steam treatment is carried out in 100% steam for 4 hours.  
1013 
A study on vanadium passivation with rare-earth compounds soluble in feedstock by 
1014 
impregnating FCC catalyst with rare-earth compounds such as lanthanum octoate, 
1015 

<<<PAGE 61>>>
 61
dysprosium and samarium naphthenates at the same time with vanadium naphthenates was 
1016 
conducted [166]. Adsorption measurements exhibited that more than 90% of the zeolite 
1017 
structure is preserved after high-temperature hydrothermal treatment when La/V = 1. It was 
1018 
found that high-resolution analytical microscopy showed that rare-earth vanadates (LaVO4 
1019 
and SmVO4) in the form of small particles are made on the surface of the catalyst grains on 
1020 
the catalyst matrix. These rare-earth vanadates were found both on the external surface and in 
1021 
the mesopores of the zeolite. The micro activity test indicated that gasoline yield was high, 
1022 
and coke and hydrogen yields remained higher than those of unpoisoned catalysts. The high 
1023 
gasoline level was due to the presence of rare-earth compounds to passivate the destructive 
1024 
effect of vanadium, while the high amounts of hydrogen and coke were due to the presence of 
1025 
vanadium which causes dehydrogenation and coke formation.  
1026 
6.2.3 Basic alkaline earth compounds as vanadium passivator 
1027 
Basic alkaline earth compounds such as calcium chloride and magnesium acetate have 
1028 
been investigated and found effective at scavenging vanadium [143,165]. The operating 
1029 
conditions are similar to those previously discussed for vanadium passivation using rare-earth 
1030 
compounds in 6.2.2, except that, in this case, the steam treatment was carried out in a 4% 
1031 
steam environment and 96% Argon. A small percentage of steam is employed, possibly 
1032 
because the cracking catalyst has undergone a certain level of deactivation before being used 
1033 
for this purpose. Therefore, it did not require a high percentage of steam. The Argon was 
1034 
used to provide an inert environment for the deactivation process. 
1035 
An investigation was conducted into the mechanism and poisoning of vanadium-
1036 
tolerant catalysts [143]. The findings show that basic alkaline earth oxides such as MgO and 
1037 
CaO are effective for vanadium scavenging. The micro-activity testing exhibited excellent 
1038 
activity retention when 20% MgO is blended with a cracking catalyst at vanadium loadings 
1039 
of 0.67% and 1.34% by volume on the catalyst. It was, however, concluded that the SOx in 
1040 

<<<PAGE 62>>>
 62
the regenerator flue gas can form a sulphate competing with vanadate formation. The degree 
1041 
of the competition is thermodynamically controlled since vanadate formation from the oxide 
1042 
expands the lattice and pores. This is similar to the observation in the case of the reaction of 
1043 
calcium oxide with sulfur oxides.  
1044 
Chao et al. [165] investigated vanadium passivation using calcium by imaging 
1045 
secondary ion mass spectroscopy. Fresh FCC catalyst was impregnated with 2300 ppm of 
1046 
nickel and 4500 ppm of vanadium using a cyclohexane solution of nickel and vanadyl 
1047 
naphthenates. Calcium and clay were introduced into the Ni and V-loaded catalyst and tested. 
1048 
The performance test showed that adding Ca/clay to the catalyst decreases the yields of coke 
1049 
and gas with increasing gasoline selectivity. The findings also indicated that calcium, an 
1050 
alkaline-earth metal, can passivate the deleterious effect of vanadium. This may be due to the 
1051 
ability of alkaline earth metals to form a stable complex with vanadium and helps prevent the 
1052 
deleterious effect of vanadium.

7 
Challenges and Prospects  
1054 
7.1 Challenges and future research and development 
1055 
Residue fluid catalytic cracking (RFCC) technology is the key to processing crude oil 
1056 
and unconventional feedstocks due to its ability to handle heavy crudes and maximise 
1057 
gasoline and propylene production. However, processing heavy and dirty crudes come with 
1058 
some challenges due to heavy metals, predominantly nickel, vanadium, sodium and iron. 
1059 
These heavy metals contribute to the following challenges: 
1060 
(i) 
Destruction of catalyst and the promotion of competing and undesirable side 
1061 
reactions: leads to the production of a large amount of hydrogen and coke.  
1062 
(ii) 
Production of a large amount of hydrogen: puts too much stress on the wet gas 
1063 
compressor in the RFCC unit, which is volume limited. 
1064 

<<<PAGE 63>>>
 63
(iii) 
Production of a large amount of coke: this upsets the reactor and regenerator heat 
1065 
balance and affects the main air blower in addition to blocking the catalyst’s active 
1066 
sites, thereby reducing the activity of the catalyst.  
1067 
Based on our analysis, future research and development should focus on the 
1068 
following: 
1069 
(i) 
Design of RFCC catalyst: RFCC catalysts that can withstand the action of metal 
1070 
poisons present in heavy crude oils should be properly designed. Vanadium destroys 
1071 
the framework of the conventional FCC catalyst by hydrolysis of the silicon and 
1072 
aluminium bonds in the zeolite structure. Nickel promotes undesirable side reactions, 
1073 
and sodium neutralises the acid sites of the catalyst, which is responsible for cracking 
1074 
the hydrocarbon feedstocks. 
1075 
(ii) 
Replacement of antimony as nickel passivator: Antimony has been employed to 
1076 
mitigate the harmful effects of nickel poison due to its ability to form Ni-Sb alloy, 
1077 
which is difficult to reduce to a more deleterious elemental nickel. However, 
1078 
antimony has recently been identified to cause NOx emissions. This constrained it due 
1079 
to stringent environmental regulations. In addition to antimony being responsible for 
1080 
the corrosion of downstream equipment due to its excessive injection, there are also 
1081 
safe handling concerns of antimony due to its toxicity. 
1082 
(iii) 
Developing an RFCC catalyst that incorporates boron as a nickel passivator: Unlike 
1083 
antimony, boron is mobile under RFCC conditions and can migrate to the immobile 
1084 
nickel on the catalyst, effectively passivating the harmful effect of nickel. Also, boron 
1085 
does not cause NOx emissions and is environmentally friendly and safe to handle. 
1086 
These positive attributes of boron make it suitable as a nickel passivator and can 
1087 
therefore be incorporated into the catalyst mix during the synthesis process. 
1088 

<<<PAGE 64>>>
 64
(iv) 
More research into the passivation of vanadium. Vanadium is regarded as the most 
1089 
harmful of all catalyst poisons. In addition to promoting dehydrogenation reactions, 
1090 
vanadium causes hydrolysis of the silicon and aluminium bond of the zeolite catalyst, 
1091 
which leads to the collapse of the catalyst structure. To date, tin-based compounds 
1092 
have been identified as having the ability to mitigate the harmful effect of vanadium, 
1093 
but there has not been much research in this area. Therefore, more research focus is 
1094 
needed in the area. 
1095 
8 
Conclusions  
1096 
The Fluid Catalytic Cracking (FCC) process has been an area of interest to petroleum 
1097 
refiners due to its ability to convert the bottom of the barrel into more useful products leading 
1098 
to an increase in refinery margins. The change in crude oil quality towards heavier and 
1099 
unconventional crudes has necessitated the invention of Residue Fluid Catalytic Cracking 
1100 
(RFCC) technology along with its associated catalysts, which can withstand the high coke 
1101 
precursor (Conradson Carbon Residue) and heavy metals such as vanadium and nickel that 
1102 
come with the heavy and dirty crudes. Conradson Carbon Residue (CCR) generates a large 
1103 
amount of coke, leading to high temperature in the regenerator when this coke is burnt off the 
1104 
catalyst during the regeneration process. Nickel and vanadium promote undesirable side 
1105 
reactions such as dehydrogenation and destruction of the cracking catalyst by hydrolysis of 
1106 
the silica-alumina bond, respectively. This review summarises nickel and vanadium 
1107 
mitigation strategies, the most important metals that affect the cracking catalyst. The 
1108 
advantages and drawbacks of nickel and vanadium mitigation strategies were evaluated. 
1109 
Antimony has been extensively reported to be successful at mitigating the harmful effect of 
1110 
nickel. Still, it causes NOx emission and corrosion of equipment due to excessive injection. 
1111 
There are also safe handling concerns. Bismuth and transition alumina have been used as 
1112 
replacements for antimony, but bismuth is not very effective, and transition alumina is not 
1113 

<<<PAGE 65>>>
 65
mobile under RFCC conditions. The mitigation of vanadium has not been widely studied. 
1114 
However, tin-based compounds have been proven to successfully mitigate vanadium's effect. 
1115 
As a result of the existing challenges of nickel mitigation, as discussed in this review, boron 
1116 
has been identified as an environmentally benign and economically effective mitigation 
1117 
strategy for a nickel. Boron does not cause NOx emission. It is safe to handle and mobile 
1118 
under RFCC conditions, making it a promising alternative for mitigating nickel poisoning.
