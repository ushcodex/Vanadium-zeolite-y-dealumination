## 2.1 Energy, Crude Oil, and the Strategic Place of Refining

### 2.1.1 Global Energy Landscape and Liquid Hydrocarbons
Liquid hydrocarbons derived from petroleum remain the dominant energy carriers sustaining the global industrial economy, accounting for over 31% of the world's primary energy consumption (OPEC, 2024). Despite rapid technological expansion in renewable electricity generation, battery electric mobility, and green hydrogen systems, liquid transportation fuels - specifically motor gasoline, jet aviation kerosene, and automotive gas oil (diesel) - exhibit irreplaceable volumetric energy densities (32–36 MJ/L) and existing transport distribution infrastructures that guarantee their continued dominance through the mid-21st century (Gary et al., 2007; Speight, 2014).

Crude oil extracted from subsurface reservoirs is an exceedingly complex, heterogeneous mixture of tens of thousands of distinct hydrocarbon and heteroatomic molecular species. Petroleum refining serves as the indispensable chemical manufacturing interface that separates, rearranges, cracks, and purifies these crude mixtures into tightly regulated, consumer-grade fuel specifications and petrochemical chemical building blocks (ethylene, propylene, butylenes, benzene, toluene, and xylenes) (Vogt & Weckhuysen, 2015). Over the past three decades, global crude oil production has progressively shifted toward heavier, higher-density, and more contaminated reserves. This secular shift has forced modern conversion refineries to evolve from simple hydroskimming configurations into complex deep-conversion facilities capable of upgrading heavy residual fractions (atmospheric tower bottoms and vacuum residues) into clean, low-emissions light distillates (Sadeghbeigi, 2012).

### 2.1.2 Nigerian Refining Landscape and Transition to RFCC
The Federal Republic of Nigeria possesses the largest proven natural gas reserves (over 206 trillion cubic feet) and the second-largest crude oil reserves (approximately 37 billion barrels) in Africa (OPEC, 2024). Historically, Nigeria’s petroleum sector exhibited a severe, paradoxical structural disequilibrium: while producing between 1.3 and 2.2 million barrels per day of high-quality light sweet crude oil, the domestic downstream sector suffered prolonged functional collapse. The nation’s four state-owned refineries, operated by subsidiaries of the Nigerian National Petroleum Company Limited (NNPC Ltd) - comprising the Old Port Harcourt Refinery (60,000 bpd), the New Port Harcourt Refinery (150,000 bpd), the Warri Refining and Petrochemical Company (WRPC, 125,000 bpd), and the Kaduna Refining and Petrochemical Company (KRPC, 110,000 bpd) - operated at near-zero capacity utilization for nearly two decades due to operational obsolescence, pipeline vandalism, turnaround maintenance deferrals, and political instability (allAfrica, 2025; Punch, 2025). Consequently, Nigeria became almost entirely dependent on importing finished petroleum products, consuming an estimated 60 to 70 million liters of imported premium motor spirit (PMS) daily at immense fiscal cost in foreign exchange depletion and fuel subsidy allocations (Adanenche et al., 2023; Petroleum Industry Act, 2021).

The domestic downstream paradigm was fundamentally disrupted by the passage of the Petroleum Industry Act (PIA, 2021) and the commissioning of the 650,000 barrels per day grassroots Dangote Petroleum Refinery in the Lekki Free Trade Zone, Lagos. Engineered as the largest single-train crude distillation facility in the world, the refinery's secondary conversion block is anchored upon a massive 218,000 barrels per day Residue Fluid Catalytic Cracking (RFCC) unit (Leadership, 2026; Nigerian Eye, 2026). In parallel, NNPC Ltd embarked on comprehensive rehabilitation contracts to bring the Port Harcourt, Warri, and Kaduna facilities back online (NS Energy, 2021; Punch, 2025).

## 2.2 Petroleum Chemistry and Crude Oil Residues

### 2.2.1 Hydrocarbon and Heteroatom Distributions
Petroleum crude oils are classified chemically using SARA fraction analysis into four broad solubility classes: Saturates, Aromatics, Resins, and Asphaltenes (Speight, 2014):
1. **Saturates (Aliphatics):** Normal alkanes ($n$-paraffins), branched alkanes (iso-paraffins), and cycloalkanes (naphthenes). Saturates crack readily via carbenium ion mechanisms over acidic zeolites, yielding high-octane branched gasoline and light olefins.
2. **Aromatics:** Hydrocarbons containing one or more benzene rings, ranging from simple alkylbenzenes to fused polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) like naphthalenes, phenanthrenes, and pyrenes.
3. **Resins:** Polar, heterocyclic compounds containing nitrogen, oxygen, and sulphur atoms, exhibiting molecular weights between 500 and 1200 g/mol, which serve as natural surfactants dispersing asphaltenes in crude oil.
4. **Asphaltenes:** The heaviest, most refractory fraction, defined operationally as insoluble in $n$-pentane or $n$-heptane but soluble in toluene. Asphaltenes consist of condensed polyaromatic core sheets substituted with aliphatic side chains, carrying abundant heteroatoms ($N, S, O$) and chelated heavy transition metal complexes (Speight, 2014).

### 2.2.2 Heavy Residue Characterization and Metalloporphyrins
During atmospheric and vacuum distillation, volatile hydrocarbons boil off, leaving non-volatile, high-molecular-weight species concentrated within the atmospheric residue (ATB) and vacuum residue (VR). In RFCC operations, feeding atmospheric residue introduces heavy organometallic contaminants that poison cracking catalysts.

The primary transition metals found in crude petroleum are **vanadium ($V$)** and **nickel ($Ni$)**, with secondary concentrations of iron ($Fe$), copper ($Cu$), and sodium ($Na$) (Mitchell, 1980; Occelli, 1991b). These metals exist predominantly as **metalloporphyrins** - macrocyclic, aromatic tetrapyrrole rings coordinating a central divalent or tetravalent metal ion - as well as non-porphyrin petroporphyrin complexes associated with asphaltenic sheets (Speight, 2014). Vanadium in virgin crude exists primarily in the +4 oxidation state as the vanadyl ion, $\text{VO}^{2+}$, complexed within vanadyl porphyrins ($\text{VO-N}_4\text{C}_{32}\text{H}_{36}$).

While Nigerian crudes (e.g., Bonny Light, Escravos, Brass River) are globally classified as light, sweet crudes ($API > 35^{\circ}$, sulphur $< 0.2\text{ wt}\%$), their residual fractions concentrate significant quantities of heavy metals. When an RFCC unit processes 218,000 barrels per day of residue containing even 5–15 ppm of vanadium, several hundred kilograms of metallic vanadium are deposited onto the circulating catalyst inventory every 24 hours. Because metals do not volatilize at riser temperatures, they accumulate irreversibly on the catalyst, reaching equilibrium catalyst (Ecat) metal loadings of 1,500 to 5,000 ppmw V unless compensated by continuous fresh catalyst addition (Adanenche et al., 2023; Etim et al., 2018).

## 2.3 The Fluid Catalytic Cracking (FCC) Process

### 2.3.1 Process Flow and Reaction Chemistry
The Fluid Catalytic Cracking process operates on the principle of fluidized solids circulation (Levenspiel, 1999; Sadeghbeigi, 2012). Figure 2.1 illustrates the typical industrial configuration of an RFCC riser-regenerator loop.

```
       [Flue Gas to CO Boiler / Cyclones]
                    ^
                    |
           +-----------------+
           |   REGENERATOR   | <--- Combustion Air Blast
           |  (950 - 1050 K) |
           |  Coke Burnoff   |
           +-----------------+
              |           ^
    Regenerated           Spent
    Catalyst              Catalyst
    Slide Valve           Slide Valve
              |           |
              v           |
           +-----------------+
           |   RISER REACTOR |
           |   (770 - 820 K) |
           |   1 - 3 seconds |
           +-----------------+
              ^           |
              |           v
    [Residue Feed]     [To Main Fractionator:
     + Atomizing        Gas, Naphtha, LCO, HCO]
     Steam
```
**Figure 2.1**  
*Simplified schematic flow diagram of an RFCC riser-regenerator closed circulation system.*

In the riser reactor, preheated residue feed ($550–620\text{ K}$) is atomized with high-velocity dispersion steam (1–3 wt% of feed) into a rising stream of hot, regenerated catalyst ($950–1030\text{ K}$). Upon contact, the hydrocarbon feed instantly vaporizes, expanding the vapor volume by a factor of 100 and propelling the fluidized catalyst suspension upward through the tubular riser at velocities of 10–25 m/s. The contact time is extremely short, typically 1.0 to 3.0 seconds, terminating in high-efficiency ballistic separation cyclones at the riser top that rapidly disengage hydrocarbon vapors from spent catalyst particles to prevent secondary overcracking into dry gas ($CH_4, C_2H_6$) and coking (Sadeghbeigi, 2012; Vogt & Weckhuysen, 2015).

Cracking proceeds via heterogeneous carbenium ion mechanisms catalyzed by zeolitic Brønsted acid sites (Corma & Orchillés, 2000). The initiation step occurs either by protonation of an alkene (forming a carbenium ion) or by direct protonation of a paraffin at elevated temperature over strong Brønsted acid sites, forming a penta-coordinated carbonium ion that rapidly cleaves into a carbenium ion and molecular hydrogen (or a small alkane). The carbenium ions propagate catalytic cracking through three primary elementary mechanisms:
1. **$\beta$-Scission:** Cleavage of a carbon-carbon single bond located $\beta$ to the trivalent positively charged carbon, generating a smaller carbenium ion and a terminal alkene (olefin).
2. **Hydride Transfer:** Intermolecular transfer of a hydride ion ($H^-$) from an unactivated alkane to a carbenium ion, creating a new carbenium ion while quenching the former.
3. **Isomerization:** Rapid skeletal branching via methide and hydride shifts, yielding tertiary carbenium ions that impart high octane ratings (RON $> 92$) to the resulting FCC gasoline (Corma & Orchillés, 2000; Venuto & Habib, 1979).

### 2.3.2 Riser-Regenerator Dynamics and Equilibrium Catalyst (Ecat)
During cracking, highly aromatic, hydrogen-deficient carbonaceous residue ("coke") deposits on the catalyst (0.8–2.0 wt% of catalyst mass). This coke blocks the zeolite micropores and neutralizes Brønsted acid sites, causing immediate deactivation. Spent catalyst is separated, stripped of entrained volatile hydrocarbons in a counter-current steam stripping zone (750–800 K), and directed down a standpipe into the regenerator.

In the regenerator, an air blast fluidizes the spent catalyst at 950–1050 K and 0.15–0.35 MPa, burning off coke via exothermic oxidation:

$$\text{C (coke)} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 \quad (\Delta H = -393.5\text{ kJ/mol})$$
$$2\text{C (coke)} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO} \quad (\Delta H = -221.0\text{ kJ/mol})$$
$$\text{H}_2\text{ (coke)} + \frac{1}{2}\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O (steam)} \quad (\Delta H = -241.8\text{ kJ/mol})$$

This combustion generates substantial thermal energy, elevating the catalyst temperature to 970–1050 K. The hot, regenerated catalyst flows back into the riser base, supplying the sensible heat of vaporization and endothermic heat of reaction ($\approx 350–550\text{ kJ/kg}$) required to crack fresh feed, establishing a closed heat-balanced loop (Sadeghbeigi, 2012).

Because catalysts continuously circulate through this thermal cycle hundreds of times per day, catalyst particles exhibit an age distribution ranging from hours to months. The circulating inventory is termed **Equilibrium Catalyst (Ecat)**. Maintaining acceptable Ecat activity and metals tolerance requires continuous daily withdrawal of aged Ecat and replacement with fresh, uncontaminated catalyst (fresh addition rates typically 0.5 to 2.5 kg catalyst per metric ton of feed) (Vogt & Weckhuysen, 2015).

## 2.4 Zeolite Y and FCC Catalyst Architecture

### 2.4.1 Faujasite Framework Structure and Brønsted Acidity
The catalytic engine within an FCC microsphere is synthetic **zeolite Y**, possessing the crystallographic framework topology of the natural mineral **faujasite (FAU)** (Breck, 1974; Baerlocher & McCusker, n.d.).

```
           +---------------------------------------------+
           |           FAUJASITE (FAU) TOPOLOGY          |
           |                                             |
           |         Sodalite Cages (cubooctahedra)      |
           |                  \      /                   |
           |                   [D6R]                     |
           |                  /      \                   |
           |               Supercage (12-ring)           |
           |             Free Diameter = 0.74 nm         |
           |             Cavity Volume = 1.20 nm         |
           +---------------------------------------------+
```
**Figure 2.2**  
*Crystallographic hierarchy of faujasite zeolite Y showing sodalite cages linked via hexagonal prisms into large 12-ring supercages.*

The faujasite framework belongs to the cubic space group $Fd\bar{3}m$ with a unit cell parameter $a_0 \approx 2.485\text{ nm}$ in pristine sodium-Y form, containing 192 tetrahedral T-atoms ($Si$ or $Al$) per unit cell. The structural hierarchy consists of truncated octahedra (sodalite or $\beta$-cages, containing 24 T-atoms) linked tetrahedrally through double 6-rings (hexagonal prisms, D6R) to enclose large, quasi-spherical **supercages** (internal cavity diameter $\approx 1.2\text{ nm}$, accessible via four tetrahedrally oriented 12-membered oxygen rings with a crystallographic free aperture of $0.74\text{ nm}$) (Breck, 1974; Sauer, 1989). This three-dimensional, interconnected pore architecture permits rapid molecular diffusion of hydrocarbon feed molecules into the supercages and unrestricted egress of cracked products.

In an all-silica framework ($\text{SiO}_2$), the lattice is electrically neutral because each silicon cation ($Si^{4+}$) is coordinated tetrahedrally to four bridging oxygen atoms ($O^{2-}$). In zeolite Y, isomorphic substitution of a tetravalent silicon cation by a trivalent aluminium cation ($Al^{3+}$) generates a formal negative charge ($-1$) localized over the four coordinated framework oxygen atoms:

$$\left[ \text{AlO}_{4/2} \right]^{-1}$$

To maintain macroscopic electrical neutrality, a mobile, non-framework counter-cation must balance this charge. When the counter-cation is a proton ($H^+$), it coordinates to one of the bridging framework oxygens, forming a bridging hydroxyl group ($Si-O(H)-Al$), as illustrated in Figure 2.3.

```
                   H  (Brønsted Proton)
                   :
                   O (Bridging Oxygen)
                 /   \
               Si     Al (Trivalent Framework Atom)
              /|\     /|\
             O O O   O O O  (Framework Oxygen Atoms)
```
**Figure 2.3**  
*Local chemical architecture of the classical bridging Brønsted acid hydroxyl site ($Si-O(H)-Al$) in faujasite zeolite Y.*

This bridging hydroxyl group represents the classical zeolitic **Brønsted acid site**. Due to the strong electronegativity difference and inductive electron withdrawal by adjacent framework silicon atoms, the $O-H$ bond is strongly polarized, imparting high acid strength comparable to liquid superacids (Hammett acidity $H_0 \approx -8$ to $-12$) (Corma & Orchillés, 2000; Sauer, 1989).

In the faujasite unit cell, there are four crystallographically non-equivalent oxygen positions designated $O_1, O_2, O_3,$ and $O_4$:
- **$O_1$:** Points directly into the open 12-ring supercage, presenting zero steric hindrance to reacting hydrocarbons.
- **$O_2$:** Located on the sodalite cage aperture, accessible to small molecules.
- **$O_3$:** Bridges sodalite cages and double 6-rings, oriented toward the internal channels.
- **$O_4$:** Resides within the hexagonal prisms, inaccessible to bulky hydrocarbons (Breck, 1974).

The Brønsted acid hydroxyl group investigated in this study is anchored at the accessible $O_1$ position, which governs catalytic cracking and initial poison adsorption.

### 2.4.2 Ultra-Stable Y (USY) and Rare-Earth Y (REY) Modifications
Pristine synthetic sodium zeolite Y (Na-Y) has a framework $Si/Al$ atomic ratio of approximately 2.4 to 2.8, containing 50 to 56 aluminium atoms per unit cell. While Na-Y possesses a high concentration of acid sites, its thermal and hydrothermal stability is poor: exposure to steam above 770 K triggers rapid collapse of the crystalline lattice into amorphous silica-alumina (Breck, 1974; Scherzer, 1989).

To survive the rigorous thermal environment of commercial FCC regenerators (950–1050 K in steam), catalyst manufacturers subject Na-Y to controlled hydrothermal modification, producing **Ultra-Stable Y (USY)**:
1. **Ammonium Ion Exchange:** Synthetic Na-Y is washed with ammonium nitrate ($\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3$) solution, exchanging sodium cations ($Na^+$) for ammonium ions ($\text{NH}_4^+$), reducing $Na_2O$ content to $< 0.5\text{ wt}\%$.
2. **High-Temperature Steaming:** The ammonium-exchanged zeolite ($\text{NH}_4\text{-Y}$) is calcined at 770–1020 K in high-pressure steam. Steaming hydrolyzes framework $Al-O$ bonds, liberating approximately 50–70% of the framework aluminium into the pores as non-framework extra-framework aluminium (EFAL) species ($AlOOH, Al(OH)_3$).
3. **Lattice Healing:** Silicon atoms migrate from defect zones into the vacant tetrahedral vacancies left by extracted aluminium, healing framework defects and shrinking the cubic unit cell parameter from $a_0 = 2.485\text{ nm}$ down to $2.425–2.435\text{ nm}$ (Scherzer, 1989).

This unit cell shrinkage dramatically increases the framework $Si/Al$ ratio of the residual crystalline lattice ($Si/Al_{\text{framework}} \approx 6–30$). According to Sanderson electronegativity theory, increasing the $Si/Al$ ratio decreases the spatial density of Brønsted acid sites, eliminating mutual acid site interference and increasing the intrinsic acid strength of the remaining isolated Brønsted sites (Corma & Orchillés, 2000; Venuto & Habib, 1979).

In heavy feed and residual cracking operations, **Rare-Earth Y (REY or RE-USY)** is frequently utilized. Catalyst manufacturers exchange trivalent lanthanide cations (principally Lanthanum, $La^{3+}$, and Cerium, $Ce^{3+}$) into the zeolite. Because trivalent rare-earth cations have high charge-to-radius ratios, they migrate upon calcination into the small sodalite cages (Site $SI'$), where they coordinate simultaneously to multiple framework oxygen atoms from adjacent sodalite rings. This coordination acts as an electrostatic "molecular clamp," stabilizing the crystalline framework, suppressing hydrothermal dealumination, and preserving high catalyst activity (Du et al., 2015; Occelli, 1996; Salahudeen et al., 2017). However, as reviewed in Section 2.7, this high rare-earth content makes REY catalysts acutely vulnerable to catastrophic structural collapse in the presence of contaminant vanadium (Pine, 1990; Wormsbecher et al., 1986).

## 2.5 Catalyst Deactivation Mechanisms in FCC/RFCC

### 2.5.1 Coking and Reversible Deactivation
During the cracking reaction in the riser, high-molecular-weight aromatic precursors, asphaltenes, and polycyclic alkenes undergo sequential condensation, oligomerization, and hydrogen transfer reactions on the active surfaces, forming a hydrogen-deficient carbonaceous deposit termed **coke** (0.8–2.0 wt% of catalyst mass) (Guisnet & Magnoux, 2001; Vogt & Weckhuysen, 2015).

FCC coke is classified into four operational components:
- **Catalytic Coke:** Formed by the intrinsic acid-catalyzed cracking and cyclization of hydrocarbons over active Brønsted acid sites.
- **Contaminant (Metals) Coke:** Formed by excessive, unselective dehydrogenation reactions catalyzed by deposited heavy metals (primarily metallic nickel and iron).
- **Conradson Carbon (Feed) Coke:** Heavy, unvaporized polyaromatic asphaltenes that physically deposit onto the external surfaces and mesopores of the catalyst particle.
- **Strippable Coke:** Entrained hydrocarbon vapors that fail to be desorbed in the steam stripper (Guisnet & Magnoux, 2001; Sadeghbeigi, 2012).

Coke physically blocks the 0.74 nm micropore apertures of the zeolite Y supercages and covers Brønsted acid hydroxyl groups, causing rapid loss of conversion. However, coking is **reversible deactivation**: upon entering the regenerator, the carbonaceous deposit combusts into $CO, CO_2,$ and $H_2O$, completely restoring access to unpoisoned active sites (Sadeghbeigi, 2012).

### 2.5.2 Hydrothermal Dealumination by Steam
Unlike coking, hydrothermal dealumination constitutes **permanent, irreversible structural deactivation**. In the oxidative regenerator, steam is generated continuously by the combustion of hydrogen contained within coke (coke contains 6–9 wt% hydrogen), yielding typical steam partial pressures of 10 to 35 kPa at 950–1050 K (Sadeghbeigi, 2012).

Under these severe conditions, gaseous water molecules act as nucleophilic hydrolyzing agents that attack framework aluminium–oxygen bonds. The overall stoichiometric reaction involves the sequential rupture of four $Al-O$ bonds surrounding a framework aluminium T-site:

$$\left[ \text{Al(OSi)}_4 \right]^{-} \text{H}^{+} + 4\text{ H}_2\text{O (g)} \rightarrow \text{Al(OH)}_3\text{ (EFAL)} + 4\text{ Si-OH (silanol nest)}$$

Each extracted aluminium atom leaves behind a **silanol nest** - a vacant tetrahedral framework cavity terminated by four silanol groups ($Si-OH$). Over prolonged commercial operation, this hydrolytic dealumination causes progressive loss of Brønsted acidity and gradual unit cell shrinkage. However, pure steam dealumination is a relatively slow, manageable aging process: modern USY zeolites are engineered to reach a stable equilibrium unit cell size ($a_0 \approx 2.425–2.430\text{ nm}$) that retains 40–60% of original crystallinity over months of operation (Scherzer, 1989; Silaghi et al., 2015).

### 2.5.3 Contaminant Metals: Nickel vs. Vanadium Poisoning
When processing heavy atmospheric residues in RFCC service, contaminant metals deposit permanently on the catalyst. The chemical behaviors of nickel and vanadium are fundamentally divergent:

1. **Nickel ($Ni$):**
   Deposited nickel deposits in the zero-valent metallic ($Ni^0$) or divalent oxide ($NiO$) state. Because nickel has a high melting point ($Ni = 1728\text{ K}$, $NiO = 2228\text{ K}$) and negligible vapor pressure under regenerator conditions, it remains **immobile**, staying pinned as discrete, nanometer-scale crystallites on the outer surface and mesoporous matrix of the microsphere (Kugler & Leta, 1988). Nickel does not penetrate the zeolite micropores and does not attack the crystalline aluminosilicate framework. Instead, nickel functions as an aggressive, unselective dehydrogenation catalyst, promoting the cleavage of $C-H$ bonds over $C-C$ bonds. This dramatically increases unwanted molecular hydrogen ($H_2$) and dry gas production, which rapidly overloads the refinery's wet gas compressors and increases coke make (Mitchell, 1980; Sadeghbeigi, 2012). Nickel poisoning is commercially counteracted by adding organometallic antimony or bismuth passivators that alloy with nickel crystallites, forming catalytically inert intermetallic phases (e.g., $NiSb_x$) (Adanenche et al., 2023).

2. **Vanadium ($V$):**
   Vanadium represents a fundamentally more catastrophic poison. Unlike nickel, vanadium oxidizes under regenerator conditions into high-oxidation-state pentavalent vanadium ($V^{5+}$), forming vanadium pentoxide ($V_2O_5$) and volatile orthovanadic acid ($H_3VO_4$) (Occelli, 1991b; Wormsbecher et al., 1986). These compounds have low melting points and significant gas-phase vapor pressures, granting vanadium **extreme mobility**. Vanadium migrates dynamically through the gas phase, penetrates the interior of zeolite Y supercages, and directly destroys the crystalline zeolite framework, as reviewed in Section 2.6.

## 2.6 Chemistry and Mobility of Vanadium in the Regenerator

### 2.6.1 Deposition, Oxidation, and Vanadic Acid ($H_3VO_4$) Volatilization
When organometallic vanadyl porphyrins enter the RFCC riser, they crack and deposit vanadium onto the catalyst surface as non-volatile, reduced sub-oxides ($V^{3+}$ and $V^{4+}$, such as $V_2O_3$ and $VO_2$) (Mitchell, 1980; Speight, 2014).

Upon circulation into the regenerator, the high-temperature (950–1050 K), oxygen-rich (1–3 vol% excess $O_2$) atmosphere rapidly oxidizes these deposits to the fully oxidized pentavalent state ($V^{5+}$), forming **vanadium pentoxide ($V_2O_5$)**:

$$2\text{VO}_2 + \frac{1}{2}\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{V}_2\text{O}_5$$

Vanadium pentoxide is a low-melting acidic oxide with a bulk melting point of **963 K ($690^{\circ}\text{C}$)** (Occelli, 1991b; Wormsbecher et al., 1986). In commercial RFCC regenerators operating between 970 and 1040 K, $V_2O_5$ exists as a molten, mobile liquid phase on the catalyst surface.

Critically, the regenerator atmosphere contains 10 to 25 vol% steam ($H_2O$) produced from coke combustion. Under these hydrothermal conditions, molten vanadium pentoxide undergoes a reversible, high-temperature gas-solid equilibrium discovered by Wormsbecher, Peters, and Maselli (1986), reacting with steam to form volatile, monomeric **orthovanadic acid ($H_3VO_4$)**:

$$\text{V}_2\text{O}_5\text{ (l, s)} + 3\text{ H}_2\text{O (g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{ H}_3\text{VO}_4\text{ (g)}$$

The equilibrium vapor pressure of gaseous $H_3VO_4$ increases exponentially with temperature and steam partial pressure:

$$P_{\text{H}_3\text{VO}_4} \propto \left( P_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} \right)^{1.5} \cdot \exp\left( -\frac{\Delta H_{\text{vap}}}{RT} \right)$$

### 2.6.2 Intra-Particle and Inter-Particle Vanadium Mobility
The formation of gaseous $H_3VO_4$ fundamentally alters catalyst deactivation kinetics. While metallic nickel remains immobilized on the external particle periphery where it deposited, volatile vanadic acid exhibits rapid **intra-particle** and **inter-particle** mobility (Kugler & Leta, 1988; Wormsbecher et al., 1996):

1. **Intra-Particle Diffusion:**
   Gaseous $H_3VO_4$ has a kinetic molecular diameter of approximately $0.48–0.52\text{ nm}$, which is substantially smaller than the $0.74\text{ nm}$ aperture of the 12-membered oxygen rings defining the zeolite Y supercages. Consequently, vanadic acid molecules diffuse without steric restriction into the deepest crystalline regions of the zeolite core.
2. **Inter-Particle Migration:**
   Because $H_3VO_4$ enters the gas phase in the regenerator bed, it desorbs from aged, heavily metal-laden catalyst particles and re-adsorbs onto freshly added catalyst particles that have circulated for only hours. Microscopic secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) and synchrotron X-ray fluorescence imaging of commercial Ecats confirm that vanadium redistributes rapidly across the entire inventory, establishing an equilibrium distribution that attacks pristine fresh catalyst (Kugler & Leta, 1988; Meirer et al., 2015).

## 2.7 Experimental Evidence on Vanadium Deactivation Mechanisms

Over the past four decades, intensive experimental investigations have explored the mechanism of vanadium-induced zeolite destruction. However, the literature remains divided among several conflicting mechanistic hypotheses.

### 2.7.1 The Acid Hydrolysis Hypothesis (Wormsbecher, Trujillo)
The acid hydrolysis hypothesis, originally formulated by Wormsbecher et al. (1986) and substantially expanded by Trujillo et al. (1997), asserts that **vanadic acid ($H_3VO_4$) acts as a mobile, aggressive Brønsted acid** that chemically catalyzes the hydrolytic cleavage of framework $Al-O$ bonds.

Under this hypothesis:
- Orthovanadic acid possesses three acidic hydroxyl groups and a polar vanadyl oxygen ($V=O$).
- Upon diffusing into the supercage, $H_3VO_4$ selectively targets the bridging framework Brønsted acid site ($Si-O(H)-Al$).
- The strong acidity of $H_3VO_4$ facilitates proton transfer to framework oxygen atoms, lowering the activation energy barrier for subsequent nucleophilic bond cleavage by water or vanadyl hydroxyls.
- This acid-catalyzed hydrolysis strips framework aluminium atoms sequentially, converting tetrahedral framework aluminium ($Al^{IV}$) into extra-framework octahedral aluminium ($Al^{VI}$), forming an insoluble extra-framework aluminium vanadate complex ($\text{AlVO}_4$) (Trujillo et al., 1997):

$$\text{Al}_{\text{framework}} + \text{H}_3\text{VO}_4 \rightarrow \text{AlVO}_4 + \text{defect silanols}$$

The formation of stable, crystalline $\text{AlVO}_4$ provides a strong thermodynamic driving force that shifts the dealumination equilibrium forward, preventing aluminium from re-incorporating into the framework and triggering irreversible structural collapse.

### 2.7.2 The Silanol Attack and Matrix Collapse Hypotheses (Pine, Occelli)
In direct contrast to the acid hydrolysis model, Pine (1990) conducted detailed hydrothermal deactivation studies and proposed the **silanol condensation hypothesis**. Pine demonstrated that when pure, highly dealuminated USY zeolites containing very few framework aluminium atoms were exposed to vanadium, they suffered catastrophic loss of crystallinity despite having low acid site densities.

Pine asserted that:
- Vanadium does not attack the Brønsted acid hydroxyl group. Instead, vanadic acid condenses selectively with terminal **silanol groups ($Si-OH$)** and defect silanol nests:

$$\text{Si-OH} + (\text{HO})_3\text{VO} \rightarrow \text{Si-O-V(O)(OH)}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

- The resulting silica-vanadia surface complexes undergo extensive structural rearrangement, accelerating the viscous sintering and recrystallization of the amorphous matrix into dense, inactive quartz or cristobalite.
- Occelli (1991b, 1996) supported this view, demonstrating that the mesoporous alumina matrix plays a sacrificial protective role by reacting with vanadium to form inert aluminium vanadate prior to vanadium reaching the zeolite crystals.

### 2.7.3 The Sodium-Vanadium Synergy and Fluxing Hypothesis (Xu)
Xu, Liu, and Madon (2002) challenged both purely acid-based models by demonstrating a profound, non-linear **synergism between contaminant sodium ($Na^+$) and vanadium**. In commercial refinery operations, heavy crude residues carry trace inorganic salts ($NaCl, Na_2SO_4$) from desalting upsets.

Xu et al. demonstrated that:
- On pure protonic USY ($H\text{-USY}$) containing less than $0.05\text{ wt}\% Na_2O$, vanadium exposure at 1033 K caused only moderate surface area decline.
- However, when trace sodium was introduced (0.2–0.8 wt% $Na$), catalyst crystallinity collapsed completely within hours.
- Xu et al. argued that vanadium pentoxide reacts with basic sodium species to form low-melting sodium metavanadate ($NaVO_3$, melting point $\approx 850\text{ K}$) or sodium vanadate eutectic mixtures ($Na_2O \cdot V_2O_5$):

$$\text{Na}_2\text{O} + \text{V}_2\text{O}_5 \rightarrow 2\text{ NaVO}_3$$

- These liquid vanadate melts act as corrosive chemical **fluxes** that dissolve the silica-alumina lattice through classical dissolution-precipitation mechanisms, rather than targeted acid hydrolysis.

### 2.7.4 Porosity Collapse and Structural Studies (Etim, Faghani)
Recent high-resolution experimental investigations by Etim et al. (2016, 2018) and Faghani et al. (2024) have characterized the physical manifestations of vanadium deactivation using advanced $^{27}Al$ and $^{29}Si$ solid-state MAS NMR, nitrogen physisorption, and transmission electron microscopy:
- **Micropore Extinction:** Nitrogen adsorption isotherms show that vanadium deactivation causes a catastrophic loss of micropore volume (decreasing from $0.32\text{ cm}^3\text{/g}$ down to $< 0.05\text{ cm}^3\text{/g}$), accompanied by collapse of the $1.2\text{ nm}$ supercage cavities.
- **Coordination Shift:** $^{27}Al$ MAS NMR spectra reveal the total disappearance of the sharp tetrahedral framework peak at $+60\text{ ppm}$ ($Al^{IV}$) and the massive emergence of a broad octahedral peak at $0\text{ ppm}$ ($Al^{VI}$), corresponding to amorphous extra-framework species.
- **Unit Cell Shrinkage:** Powder XRD tracks a rapid shrinkage of the unit cell parameter ($a_0$) followed by a dramatic drop in integrated diffraction peak intensities, signifying complete amorphous transformation (Etim et al., 2016, 2018).

## 2.8 Vanadium Passivation and Trapping Technologies

### 2.8.1 Basic Alkaline Earth and Rare-Earth Trapping Mechanisms
To prevent catastrophic zeolite destruction in RFCC operations, refiners deploy commercial chemical additives termed **vanadium passivators** and **metal traps** (Adanenche et al., 2023; Faghani et al., 2024).

Because volatile $H_3VO_4$ is an acidic oxide, the universal chemical strategy behind vanadium trapping relies on **Lewis and Brønsted acid-base neutralization**:
- Trapping agents incorporate basic metal oxides ($\text{M}_x\text{O}_y$) engineered into the catalyst matrix or injected as separate microspheroidal particles.
- When volatile $H_3VO_4$ migrates through the inter-particle void spaces, it encounters the basic metal oxide before reaching the zeolite supercages.
- The trap reacts exothermically with $H_3VO_4$, forming an inert, thermally refractory metal vanadate ($\text{MVO}_4$ or $\text{M}_3(\text{VO}_4)_2$) with a melting point well above regenerator temperatures ($T_m > 1300\text{ K}$), permanently immobilizing the poison (Adanenche et al., 2023).

The primary chemical formulations deployed industrially include:
1. **Magnesium Oxides ($MgO$):** Reacts with vanadic acid to form magnesium orthovanadate:
   $$3\text{MgO} + 2\text{H}_3\text{VO}_4 \rightarrow \text{Mg}_3(\text{VO}_4)_2 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (T_m = 1393\text{ K})$$
2. **Calcium & Barium Oxides ($CaO, BaO, BaTiO_3$):** Highly basic alkaline earth oxides that react irreversibly with vanadium to form refractory titanates and vanadates, preventing eutectic flux formation (Faghani et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2016).
3. **Rare-Earth Oxides ($\text{La}_2\text{O}_3, \text{CeO}_2, \text{LaPO}_4$):** Lanthanum oxide reacts with $H_3VO_4$ to form lanthanum orthovanadate ($\text{LaVO}_4$):
   $$\text{La}_2\text{O}_3 + 2\text{H}_3\text{VO}_4 \rightarrow 2\text{LaVO}_4 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (T_m > 2000\text{ K})$$
   Lanthanum vanadate is exceptionally stable, completely immobilizing vanadium up to 1300 K (Du et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2025).

### 2.8.2 Industrial Trapping Strategies in Modern Refineries
Commercial vanadium mitigation is deployed via two engineering configurations:
- **Integral Traps:** Basic trapping components are co-formulated directly within the cracking catalyst microsphere during spray drying, embedded in the active alumina matrix surrounding the zeolite crystals.
- **Separate Additive Particles:** Highly concentrated trapping particles are blended physically into the circulating Ecat inventory (typically 5 to 15 wt% of inventory), scavenging gas-phase $H_3VO_4$ as it desorbs in the regenerator (Adanenche et al., 2023; Sadeghbeigi, 2012).

## 2.9 Quantum Chemical and DFT Modeling in Zeolite Catalysis

### 2.9.1 Fundamentals of Density Functional Theory
Quantum mechanical simulations based on **Density Functional Theory (DFT)** have emerged as the foundational tool for probing molecular mechanisms in heterogeneous catalysis (Cramer, 2004; Jensen, 2017; Sholl & Steckel, 2009). Traditional wave function methods (e.g., Hartree-Fock, Configuration Interaction, Coupled Cluster) solve the non-relativistic time-independent Schrödinger equation:

$$\hat{H}\Psi = E\Psi$$

For a many-electron system of $N$ electrons, the wave function $\Psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2, \dots, \mathbf{r}_N)$ depends upon $3N$ spatial coordinates and $N$ spin coordinates, making exact solutions computationally intractable for zeolitic systems containing dozens or hundreds of atoms.

Density Functional Theory circumvents this exponential scaling through the foundational theorems of **Hohenberg and Kohn (1964)**:
1. **First Theorem:** The ground-state electronic energy ($E_0$) and all electronic properties of a many-electron system are uniquely determined by the ground-state electron density distribution, $\rho(\mathbf{r})$, which depends upon only **three spatial coordinates** ($x, y, z$), regardless of the number of electrons.
2. **Second Theorem:** The true ground-state electron density minimizes the total electronic energy functional, $E[\rho]$, providing a variational framework for determining the exact ground-state density:

$$E[\rho] \ge E_0[\rho_0]$$

**Kohn and Sham (1965)** mapped this intractable interacting many-body problem onto a fictitious reference system of non-interacting electrons moving in an effective local external potential ($v_{\text{eff}}$), yielding the Kohn-Sham total energy functional:

$$E_{\text{KS}}[\rho] = T_s[\rho] + \int \rho(\mathbf{r}) v_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}) d\mathbf{r} + J[\rho] + E_{\text{xc}}[\rho]$$

where:
- $T_s[\rho]$ is the kinetic energy of the non-interacting electron reference system:
  $$T_s[\rho] = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^N \langle \psi_i | \nabla^2 | \psi_i \rangle$$
- $\int \rho(\mathbf{r}) v_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}) d\mathbf{r}$ is the classical electrostatic attraction between electrons and nuclei.
- $J[\rho]$ is the classical Hartree electron-electron electrostatic repulsion:
  $$J[\rho] = \frac{1}{2} \iint \frac{\rho(\mathbf{r})\rho(\mathbf{r}')}{|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|} d\mathbf{r} d\mathbf{r}'$$
- $E_{\text{xc}}[\rho]$ is the **exchange-correlation functional**, which captures all quantum many-body non-classical electron-electron exchange, dynamic correlation, and the kinetic energy difference between interacting and non-interacting systems (Kohn & Sham, 1965; Sholl & Steckel, 2009).

### 2.9.2 Exchange-Correlation Approximations and Dispersion Corrections
Because the exact analytical form of $E_{\text{xc}}[\rho]$ is unknown, quantum chemical accuracy depends upon the choice of exchange-correlation functional approximation, organized conceptually along "Jacob's Ladder" (Jensen, 2017):
1. **Local Density Approximation (LDA):** Depends solely on local electron density, $\rho(\mathbf{r})$; severely overbinds molecules.
2. **Generalized Gradient Approximation (GGA):** Incorporates the local gradient of the electron density, $\nabla\rho(\mathbf{r})$ (e.g., PBE functional; Perdew et al., 1996).
3. **Meta-GGA:** Incorporates the kinetic energy density of occupied orbitals, $\tau(\mathbf{r})$.
4. **Hybrid Functionals:** Incorporates a fixed percentage of exact Hartree-Fock exchange energy ($E_x^{\text{HF}}$) computed from Kohn-Sham orbitals, eliminating self-interaction errors and delivering superior reaction barrier heights.
   - **B3LYP:** Becke's three-parameter hybrid functional with Lee-Yang-Parr correlation, containing 20% exact exchange (Becke, 1993; Lee et al., 1988):
     $$E_{\text{xc}}^{\text{B3LYP}} = E_{\text{xc}}^{\text{LDA}} + a_0 (E_x^{\text{HF}} - E_x^{\text{LDA}}) + a_x \Delta E_x^{\text{B88}} + E_c^{\text{LDA}} + a_c \Delta E_c^{\text{PW91}} + a_c (E_c^{\text{LYP}} - E_c^{\text{LDA}})$$
   - **PBE0:** A parameter-free hybrid functional containing 25% exact Hartree-Fock exchange mixed with 75% PBE exchange and 100% PBE correlation (Adamo & Barone, 1999):
     $$E_{\text{xc}}^{\text{PBE0}} = \frac{1}{4} E_x^{\text{HF}} + \frac{3}{4} E_x^{\text{PBE}} + E_c^{\text{PBE}}$$

Standard DFT functionals fail to describe long-range non-covalent London dispersion forces (van der Waals interactions) originating from dynamic, correlated electron fluctuations. In zeolite catalysis, dispersion forces contribute 30–70% of the net adsorption energy of molecules within microporous cages (Van Speybroeck et al., 2015). This limitation is rigorously resolved by applying **Grimme’s empirical dispersion corrections with Becke-Johnson damping ($D3(BJ)$)** (Grimme et al., 2010, 2011):

$$E_{\text{DFT-D3(BJ)}} = E_{\text{DFT-KS}} - \sum_{A < B} \left[ \frac{s_6 C_{6,AB}}{R_{AB}^6 + [R_0(AB)]^6} + \frac{s_8 C_{8,AB}}{R_{AB}^8 + [R_0(AB)]^8} \right]$$

where $C_{6,AB}$ and $C_{8,AB}$ are computed dispersion coefficients and $R_0(AB)$ is the Becke-Johnson rational damping function that smoothly cuts off dispersion at short interatomic bonding distances, eliminating unphysical over-attraction.

### 2.9.3 Cluster Models vs. Periodic Boundary Conditions
In computational catalysis, solid zeolites are represented using two distinct modeling paradigms:
1. **Periodic Boundary Conditions (PBC):** The entire crystallographic unit cell is repeated infinitely in three dimensions. For zeolite Y, the primitive rhombohedral cell contains 144 atoms, while the full cubic unit cell contains 576 atoms ($Fd\bar{3}m$). While PBC captures long-range lattice confinement and electrostatic Madelung potentials, geometry optimization and analytical Hessian frequency calculations of transition states containing transition metals are computationally prohibitive, restricting investigations to low-level GGA functionals (PBE) with small basis sets (Silaghi et al., 2015, 2016).
2. **Molecular Cluster Models:** A localized fragment centered upon the active catalytic site is cut from the experimental crystal structure, terminating broken framework $Si-O$ bonds with hydrogen atoms ($Si-H$) oriented along the original crystallographic vectors (Sauer, 1989; Van Speybroeck et al., 2015). The cluster model captures 85–95% of the local chemical reactivity, orbital hybridization, and Brønsted acid proton transfer thermodynamics. Most importantly, cluster models enable the application of high-level hybrid functionals ($B3LYP, PBE0$), large polarized triple-$\zeta$ basis sets (def2-TZVP), and analytical frequency calculations that are indispensable for rigorously verifying transition states.

Prior computational studies of zeolite dealumination have focused predominantly on pure steam hydrolysis. Malola, Svelle, Bleken, and Swang (2012) mapped steam-induced dealumination and desilication pathways in chabazite (CHA) and ZSM-5 (MFI) using DFT, identifying sequential $Al-O$ bond cleavages. Silaghi, Chizallet, Sauer, and Raybaud (2015, 2016) executed periodic DFT calculations on mordenite and chabazite, establishing that steam hydrolysis of the first $Al-O$ bond exhibits activation barriers between **76 and 125 kJ/mol**, correlating with a Brønsted-Evans-Polanyi relationship governed by framework regioselectivity. For vanadium, Tielens and Dzwigaj (2010a, 2010b) explored isolated vanadium sites in dealuminated Si-beta zeolites, focusing on oxidation-reduction spectroscopy rather than framework destruction. No published study has investigated the complete elementary reaction pathway of vanadic acid-induced framework destruction in zeolite Y.

## 2.11 Summary of Knowledge Gaps and Positioning of this Study

A rigorous synthesis of the international and domestic literature reveals four critical, unresolved knowledge gaps:

1. **Absence of Competitive Adsorption Thermodynamics:**
   No published quantum chemical investigation has quantified the competitive adsorption energy ($\Delta E_{\text{ads}}$) of $H_3VO_4$ versus $H_2O$ at the faujasite Brønsted site. While empirical studies speculate that vanadium outcompetes steam, the thermodynamic driving force has never been calculated.
2. **Unresolved Elementary Reaction Coordinate for Vanadium Attack:**
   The specific sequence of bond breaking during vanadic acid attack remains unknown. Does proton transfer precede $Al-O$ bond rupture? Which framework bond breaks first? What is the activation barrier ($\Delta E^{\ddagger}$) of the transition state?
3. **Lack of a Unified Benchmark on an Identical Model:**
   Previous computational studies of steam dealumination and vanadium species employed disparate cluster sizes, functionals, and periodic approximations, making direct comparison impossible. This study places both reaction pathways onto a single, consistent electronic energy scale.
4. **Disconnection Between Molecular Energetics and Industrial Passivator Design:**
   Industrial metal traps are deployed based on trial-and-error Ecat formulations. Resolving the binding affinity and transition state structure provides the atomistic rationale required to engineer advanced, selective metal passivators.

This project positions itself precisely at the intersection of these four gaps, deploying multi-tier quantum chemical simulations to establish the first complete, first-principles reaction mechanism of vanadic acid-induced dealumination in zeolite Y.
