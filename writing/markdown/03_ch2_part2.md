## 2.9 Vanadium Chemistry in the FCC Unit

### 2.9.1 How vanadium arrives and deposits

Vanadium enters the unit chelated in vanadyl porphyrins and related non-porphyrin complexes dissolved in the heavy part of the feed. In the riser these molecules crack or adsorb on the catalyst and decompose, leaving the metal behind on the solid (Mitchell, 1980; Kugler and Leta, 1988). Mitchell (1980) established the laboratory method, now called Mitchell impregnation (MIV), that reproduces this deposition by impregnating fresh catalyst with vanadyl naphthenate and calcining it, and it remains the standard way to prepare vanadium-contaminated catalysts for study. At deposition the vanadium is in its +4 oxidation state inside the vanadyl (VO2+) unit, but the oxidizing, high-temperature regenerator converts it progressively toward the +5 state (Occelli, 1991b).

### 2.9.2 The oxidation ladder and the two regenerator species

Vanadium is a redox metal with accessible oxidation states from +2 to +5. In the regenerator, the dominant solid product of complete oxidation is vanadium pentoxide (V2O5), whose melting point of approximately 690 °C lies inside the regenerator operating window (Faghani et al., 2024). In the presence of steam, V2O5 volatilizes according to the equilibrium measured by Wormsbecher, Peters, and Maselli (1986):

V2O5(s) + 3 H2O(g) ⇌ 2 H3VO4(v)

The product H3VO4, orthovanadic acid, is a strong acid structurally analogous to phosphoric acid. Under a representative regenerator condition of 730 °C, 20 percent steam, and a total pressure of 2 atm, Wormsbecher et al. (1986) computed and confirmed an equilibrium concentration of mobile H3VO4 vapour of about 1 to 10 parts per million, enough to contact every particle in the fluidized bed daily. Under reducing excursions, V2O5 can step back toward V2O4 and V2O3 without zeolite damage, which is why the oxidizing, wet, hot corner of the operating map is the dangerous one (Occelli, 1996).

### 2.9.3 Vanadium mobility

Experimental microscopy has repeatedly shown that contaminant vanadium spreads. Kugler and Leta (1988) imaged vanadium redistribution across equilibrium catalyst particles by secondary ion mass spectrometry. Wormsbecher, Cheng, Kim, and Harding (1996) demonstrated systematically that vanadium migrates from particle to particle during fluid-bed aging, and the trapping studies of Etim et al. (2018) confirmed vanadium appearing inside deliberately added scavenger particles. Three overlapping transport modes are recognized in the reviews: vapour transport by H3VO4, surface flow of molten V2O5 and low-melting sodium-vanadium mixed oxides, and solid state diffusion of vanadyl species into zeolite channels (Occelli, 1991b; Trujillo et al., 1997; Bai et al., 2019). This mobility is the reason a single contaminated particle does not simply die alone but seeds its neighbours with poison.

## 2.10 The Mechanism of Zeolite Y Deactivation by Vanadium: The Experimental Evidence

Five families of mechanistic evidence exist in the open literature. Each is presented below in its own terms, then compared in Table 2.2 and synthesized in Figure 2.5.

### 2.10.1 Acid hydrolysis by vanadic acid

Wormsbecher et al. (1986) showed, by combining characterization and thermodynamic analysis, that the damage agent is not solid V2O5 itself but its volatile hydrolysis product H3VO4. Being a strong acid, H3VO4 hydrolyses the aluminosilicate framework of the zeolite, cleaving the very Al-O and Si-O linkages that steam attacks, but at conditions where steam alone is comparatively sluggish. They further showed that basic solids, magnesia and calcium oxide, capture vanadium by reacting with the acid to form vanadates, protecting microactivity at vanadium loadings of 0.67 and 1.34 weight percent on catalyst. This work fixed the acid nature of the poison and the acid-base logic of all later traps.

### 2.10.2 Surface attack and catalytic kinetic role

Pine (1990) studied vanadium-contaminated ultrastable Y and exchange variants and demonstrated, kinetically, that vanadium is not consumed as it destroys: it behaves as a catalyst for the steam destruction of the zeolite, with sodium roughly equally active and the two acting synergistically. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy showed that vanadium does not penetrate far into the crystal, so the attack initiates at external surface hydroxyl groups. Significantly, vanadium also destroyed silicalite, an aluminium-free zeolite-like structure, proving that the attack can begin at Si-O bonds and silanols, not only at aluminium sites. The rate-limiting step was identified with the nucleation of the amorphous phase at the attacked surface.

### 2.10.3 Vanadyl migration, extra-framework aluminium, and internal vanadic acid

Trujillo, Uribe, Knops-Gerrits, Oviedo, and Jacobs (1997) followed vanadium speciation on zeolite Y by electron spin resonance and ultraviolet-visible diffuse reflectance. On the external surface, vanadium first sits as isolated species; heating in oxidizing atmospheres drives vanadyl (VO2+) cations into the channels, where the strongest acid sites stabilize them. Strikingly, their experiments showed that the reduced V(IV) state itself does not destroy the zeolite; water cooperates to produce vanadic acid inside the crystal according to an equilibrium they formulated, and it is this internal H3VO4 that hydrolyses the framework. They also observed that extra-framework aluminium competes for vanadium and delays its arrival at the acid sites, an observation that foreshadows modern trapping logic (Trujillo et al., 1997).

### 2.10.4 The sodium partnership

Xu, Liu, and Madon (2002) re-examined destruction as a function of sodium and vanadium together. Their data indicate that for low-sodium Y, steam hydrolysis of framework aluminium is the main route to collapse, while sodium introduces a second pathway: steam converts exchange-site sodium into surface NaOH, whose hydroxide ion attacks Si-O bonds. Vanadium's distinctive role in this picture is catalytic: mobile acidic vanadium releases cationic sodium as sodium metavanadate (NaVO3), steam hydrolyses the vanadate back to NaOH plus metavanadic acid, and the cycle repeats, so that vanadium's main sin is to keep generating fresh NaOH at the zeolite surface (Xu et al., 2002). Solid state nuclear magnetic resonance work by Hagiwara et al. (2003) independently corroborated the cooperative sodium-vanadium destruction of USY, tracking real-time changes in framework aluminium populations.

### 2.10.5 Rare earth interactions and matrix effects

Occelli (1991a, 1991b, 1996) established that rare earth stabilization, while excellent against steam, can backfire against vanadium: oxyvanadyl cations react with oxocerium species to form stable orthovanadates, stripping the stabilizing rare earth ions out of the structure and leaving the framework exposed; zeolite vanadium resistance in his series decreased as rare earth content rose, while extra-framework aluminium improved it. Yang, Chen, and Li (1994) reached complementary conclusions on vanadium-nickel interaction in REY. Later, Du et al. (2015) differentiated how rare earths are added: lanthanum introduced as separately dispersed La2O3, remaining outside the zeolite channels, traps vanadium and preserves the USY structure better than ion-exchanged lanthanum, with LaPO4 and CeO2 variants behaving differently again. Roncolatto and Lam (1998) supplied the missing mesoscale correlations across a Petrobras catalyst series: vanadium loading measurably reduced crystallinity, surface area, and unit cell size, and a greater than 50 percent loss of cumene cracking activity occurred at about 4000 ppm vanadium for the rare earth catalyst, quantifying how structural damage translates to performance loss (Roncolatto and Lam, 1998).

### 2.10.6 Where the literature agrees and where it still argues

The literature agrees on four points beyond dispute: the regenerator is where vanadium does its damage; the aggressive forms are mobile, oxidized, acidic vanadium species; the end state is aluminium loss and framework collapse of zeolite Y; and the sodium partnership aggravates every other effect (Wormsbecher et al., 1986; Trujillo et al., 1997; Xu et al., 2002; Bai et al., 2019). The literature still argues about the first bond attacked. Pine's (1990) silicalite experiments implicate surface Si-OH and Si-O bonds, Wormsbecher et al. (1986) and Trujillo et al. (1997) describe acid hydrolysis of the aluminosilicate framework with aluminium at its obvious electrophilic centre, and Xu et al. (2002) transfer much of the damage to vanadium-catalysed NaOH attacking Si-O bonds. Each claim was derived from a different catalyst formulation observed by a different technique. What no study has done is place the proposed elementary steps on a common quantum chemical energy scale for zeolite Y; the present study addresses that comparison.

## 2.11 Countermeasures Against Vanadium

### 2.11.1 Passivation and trapping chemistry

Passivation means converting the poison into an inert compound on the spot; trapping means offering the poison an alternative solid partner that binds it irreversibly away from the zeolite. For vanadium, the governing logic set by Wormsbecher et al. (1986) is acid-base neutralization: basic oxides intercept the acidic vanadium species to form high-melting vanadates (Adanenche et al., 2023). ## 2.12 Learning from the Molecule: Quantum Chemical Methods and DFT

### 2.12.1 Why compute at all

Every experimental technique in Table 2.2 reads some population average: diffraction reads average crystallinity, spectroscopy reads average speciation, microactivity testing reads average conversion. None can watch a single H3VO4 molecule enter a supercage, dock at a Brønsted site, and cut an Al-O bond. Quantum chemical calculation supplies exactly this microscope: given a model of the atoms, it returns structures and energies of complexes, intermediates, and transition structures, from which mechanisms can be compared on equal footing (Sholl and Steckel, 2009; Van Speybroeck et al., 2015).

### 2.12.2 From the Schrödinger equation to Kohn-Sham theory

Quantum chemistry seeks approximate solutions to the time-independent Schrödinger equation, which states that a Hamiltonian operator acting on a many-electron wavefunction returns the product of the energy and the wavefunction. Because exact solutions exist only for one-electron systems, approximations follow a ladder: Hartree-Fock theory treats each electron in the average field of all others but ignores electron correlation; post-Hartree-Fock theories repair that error at rapidly growing cost. Density functional theory takes the cheaper and more robust path: instead of the wavefunction, it works with the electron density, a function of only three spatial variables regardless of system size (Hohenberg and Kohn, 1964). The first Hohenberg-Kohn theorem proves that the ground state density uniquely determines all ground state properties, and the second proves that the true density is the one minimizing the total energy, giving a variational principle. Kohn and Sham (1965) made this practical by mapping the interacting electrons onto a fictitious system of non-interacting electrons with the same density, solved through self-consistent one-electron equations. All the physics the mapping hides is concentrated in one mathematical object, the exchange-correlation functional, whose exact form is unknown and must be approximated. Standard textbook treatments of these foundations and of the practical approximations that follow are given by Cramer (2004) and Jensen (2017).

### 2.12.3 The exchange-correlation ladder used in this study

Functionals form a quality ladder (Sholl and Steckel, 2009):

1. Local density approximation (LDA) uses only the density at each point; it is too crude for adsorption energies.
2. Generalized gradient approximations (GGAs), including the PBE functional of Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof (1996), add density gradients and are the workhorse of periodic solid state DFT.
3. Hybrid functionals mix a fraction of exact Hartree-Fock exchange into a GGA; B3LYP combines Becke's three-parameter exchange form (Becke, 1993) with the correlation form of Lee, Yang, and Parr (1988), while PBE0 mates PBE with one quarter exact exchange without fitted parameters (Adamo and Barone, 1999). Hybrids describe localized bonding, acidity, and reaction barriers in molecules and clusters far better than GGAs.
4. Dispersion corrections. Standard functionals miss the weak van der Waals attraction that holds molecules inside zeolite pores; Grimme's pairwise corrections fix this as an add-on, with the D3 scheme and its Becke-Johnson damping variant D3(BJ) being the community standard (Grimme et al., 2010; Grimme, Ehrlich, and Goerigk, 2011).

### 2.12.4 Basis sets, BSSE, and corrections

In finite-molecule quantum chemistry, atomic orbitals are approximated by basis sets, finite sets of mathematical functions centred on atoms. The def2 family of Ahlrichs and coworkers provides balanced split-valence and triple-zeta sets that are all-electron for elements through krypton, including silicon, aluminium, oxygen, sodium, and vanadium in this study, so no pseudopotential is required for any atom in the target systems (Weigend and Ahlrichs, 2005). When two fragments bind, each borrows the other's basis functions and gains an artificial stabilization called basis set superposition error (BSSE); the counterpoise procedure of Boys and Bernardi (1970) estimates and removes this error by recomputing each fragment's energy in the full dimer basis. Zero-point vibrational energy and thermal corrections to enthalpy and Gibbs free energy come from harmonic frequency analysis, with literature scaling factors correcting the systematic overestimation of harmonic frequencies (Alecu et al., 2010).

### 2.12.5 Cluster versus periodic models for solids

Two model philosophies exist for applying quantum chemistry to a zeolite. Periodic models repeat the full unit cell in space and capture long-range electrostatics and confinement, but at current budgets they are affordable mainly with GGA functionals. Cluster models cut a chemically meaningful fragment, such as a double six-membered ring, out of the crystal, cap the cut bonds with hydrogen atoms, and treat the fragment as a molecule in vacuum; this permits hybrid-function accuracy at the reactive site at the price of the boundary approximation (Sauer, 1989; Van Speybroeck et al., 2015). The cluster approach is the one that the DFT-on-zeolite literature has used for decades, and it permits hybrid-functional accuracy on modest computing resources at the price of the boundary approximation. It is the approach adopted here, with published periodic DFT results used for validation rather than production.

## 2.13 Prior DFT Studies Nearest to the Present Problem

### 2.13.1 DFT of zeolite dealumination

Malola, Svelle, Bleken, and Swang (2012) computed detailed reaction paths for both dealumination and desilication of protonic zeolites from density functional calculations, establishing the elementary water and silanol chemistry of framework dissolution. Silaghi, Chizallet, Petracovschi, Kerber, Sauer, and Raybaud (2015) then performed periodic DFT on faujasite and related frameworks and showed that the initiating Al-O(H) bond hydrolysis proceeds via water adsorption on aluminium followed by water dissociation over a framework oxygen, with barriers of about 76 to 125 kJ/mol depending on the aluminium's crystallographic environment; they further demonstrated a Brønsted-Evans-Polanyi relation, a linear link between reaction energy and barrier that lets one predict which site breaks first from thermodynamics alone. Silaghi, Chizallet, Sauer, and Raybaud (2016) completed the picture with the subsequent steps and the confinement of the expelled extra-framework aluminium. Directly relevant numeric anchors therefore exist for the steam-only baseline of the present study.

### 2.13.2 DFT of vanadium in and on zeolites

Tielens (2009) used DFT reactivity descriptors to probe framework vanadium, niobium, and tantalum sites in zeolitic materials, quantifying their local acidity tendencies. Tielens and Dzwigaj (2010a) carried out periodic DFT on vanadium-doped zeolite models and verified the predictions against Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, showing that V-OH groups at framework vanadium sites are more acidic than silanols and that protonation can generate stable vanadyl-containing sites; hydration was found to be almost energy-neutral, with water remaining in the cavity centre. Tielens and Dzwigaj (2010b) screened group V metal substitution into silicate models as a route to the active site. These studies prove both that DFT handles vanadium-zeolite chemistry reliably and that published DFT has so far considered vanadium as a framework dopant and redox site, not as the external deactivating agent of FCC service.

### 2.13.3 The identified gap

No located publication computes, at any level of theory, the adsorption and elementary attack energetics of regenerator vanadium species, H3VO4, VO2+, NaVO3, or NaOH, on the Brønsted-acid faujasite framework that hosts FCC cracking; nor has any located study pitted, on one energy axis, Pine's silanol attack, the Wormsbecher-Trujillo acid hydrolysis, and the Xu-Madon NaOH pathway against one another and against the steam-only benchmark of Silaghi and coworkers (2015, 2016). The gap is therefore not a shortage of data but a shortage of comparable energetics for the disputed steps, on the catalyst, in the operating window; it is the gap the present study is designed to fill.

## 2.16 Research Gap and Positioning of This Study

Assembling the chapter yields a precise gap statement:

1. Experimental mechanism studies of vanadium attack on zeolite Y are abundant but reach partly different conclusions about the first bond attacked and the role of sodium, because each observes a different compilation of atoms (Pine, 1990; Trujillo et al., 1997; Xu et al., 2002).
2. Periodic DFT has quantified steam-only dealumination energetics of faujasite with explicit barriers (Malola et al., 2012; Silaghi et al., 2015, 2016), but has not been extended to the regenerator vanadium species documented experimentally.
3. DFT vanadium-zeolite studies treat vanadium as a desirable framework dopant, not as the FCC deactivating agent (Tielens, 2009; Tielens and Dzwigaj, 2010a, 2010b).
4. Nigerian RFCC reality, including the operational record of the Dangote unit, makes mechanistic, computation-driven understanding of vanadium attack a practical national need (Leadership, 2026; Sahara Reporters, 2025), and the Zaria research programme has left this electronic-structure space open while building strength on every adjacent leg (Adanenche et al., 2023; Olanrewaju et al., 2015; Salahudeen et al., 2017; Uzochukwu et al., 2023).

The study therefore positions itself at the intersection of these four observations: a cluster-model, dispersion-corrected hybrid DFT survey of the energetics of vanadic-acid attack on a faujasite acid-site model, benchmarked against the published periodic DFT steam baseline and against the experimental record. ## 2.17 Chapter Summary

FCC and RFCC units convert heavy petroleum fractions to gasoline over zeolite Y catalysts, and Nigerian refineries depend on them. In the regenerator, feed-borne vanadium forms mobile, oxidized, acidic species, chiefly vanadic acid, which hydrolyse the faujasite framework, extract its aluminium, and collapse the crystal; sodium aggravates the process. Documented countermeasures rely on acid-base trapping of the vanadium species but remain empirical, because the elementary step energetics of the attack have not been computed on a common scale. Density functional theory supplies that scale, cluster models make hybrid-functional calculation affordable, published periodic DFT provides the steam-only benchmark, and no located study has applied the method to the regenerator vanadium species on zeolite Y. Chapter Three specifies the computational investigation designed to close that gap.
